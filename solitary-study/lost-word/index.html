Stage IX Verbum Perditum

The Lost Word

What was the purpose of initiation?

Recommended duration: about 3 months · 0 essential readings · 6 rereadings · 4 exercises ·

i. The Threshold

In the legend of the third degree, the master builder of the temple is slain, and with him is lost the true word of a Master Mason. A substitute is given in its place, and the search for what was lost is handed on to every Mason who follows.

When you began this curriculum, that legend was a story with a moral. Nothing about it has changed since. The temple is the same temple, the pillars are the same pillars, the rough ashlar is still rough and the word is still lost.

What has changed is you. You now know what the Platonists meant by the return of the soul, what the Hermeticists meant by the microcosm, what the Qabalists meant by a divine name, what the alchemists meant by the Stone and what the Rosicrucians meant by the vault. The symbols have not changed. Your understanding of them has.

ii. The Purpose

This final stage is a work of integration. Its purpose is to return to the Masonic symbols with which the curriculum began and to investigate them through every tradition you have studied: the temple as microcosm, the pillars, the altar, the rough ashlar, death and raising, the Holy Grail, the Philosopher's Stone, the Logos, the Lost Word and the Great Work (Figures 22 and 23).

The stage ends with a concluding work: you choose one initiatory symbol or allegory and investigate it through multiple philosophical and esoteric traditions, in writing, with the care of a scholar and the attention of a practitioner.

iii. Prerequisites

  • Stages I through VIII.
  • Your study journal and the list of unanswered questions you began in Stage I.
  • Before beginning, review Stage VIII, The Language of the Mysteries.

iv. The Intellectual Architecture

There are no new essential books in this stage. Instead you return to Mackey, Pike and Wilmshurst, and to Waite, Case and Hogan, reading them now with the knowledge of the intervening stages. Passages that once seemed obscure or merely rhetorical will now point to specific doctrines you have studied.

The integration diagram (Figure 23) arranges the whole curriculum as a circle that returns to its beginning. Each stage contributes an instrument for reading the Craft: Platonic philosophy explains the temple as an image of order, Hermeticism explains it as an image of the person, the Qabalah supplies the divine names and the pillars, alchemy and Rosicrucianism supply the doctrine of regeneration, ceremonial magic supplies the theory of ritual, and the Tarot supplies a method of reading images.

The Grail, the Stone and the Lost Word appear together here because three traditions have used them for a similar idea: a lost or hidden perfection whose recovery restores the seeker. They are studied as parallel symbols. Their parallel is a comparative interpretation, and this stage asks you to state carefully what the comparison does and does not establish.

Kabbalah & Qabalah

Hermetic Qabalah tradition · Stage V

The Qabalah of the Western occult revival, developed by Lévi and the Golden Dawn as an organizing system for magic and the Tarot.

Alchemy & Rosicrucianism

The Holy Grail concept · Stage IX

The vessel of the medieval romances, sought and attained only by the prepared seeker. See the Objects of Power dossier.

Alchemy tradition · Stage VI

The art of transmutation: a laboratory practice with a long tradition of spiritual interpretation and, later, psychological reading.

  • Documented historical influence → The Philosopher's Stone: The Stone is the goal of the alchemical work.
  • Documented historical influence → Rosicrucianism: The Chymical Wedding is an alchemical allegory; alchemical language pervades the Rosicrucian literature.
The Philosopher's Stone concept · Stage VI

The goal of the alchemical work, read spiritually as the regenerated human being.

  • Philosophical relationship → The Great Work: The Stone is the completion of the Great Work.
The Great Work concept · Stage IX

The alchemical opus, and by extension the whole work of interior transformation.

  • Educational prerequisite → The Lost Word: The final stage returns to the Masonic symbols with the doctrine of regeneration (Stage VI to IX).
Rosicrucianism tradition · Stage VI

The current launched by the manifestos of 1614–1616: a hidden fraternity, the vault of Christian Rosenkreutz and the regeneration of humanity.

  • Comparative symbolic relationship → Freemasonry: Rosicrucian themes appear in several Masonic degrees; the historical relationship between the manifestos and early speculative Freemasonry is uncertain.
Arthur Edward Waite author · Stage VI

Waite is both a historian of the Rosicrucian current and the designer of the most influential occult Tarot. His scholarship is cautious about extravagant claims of lineage, which makes him an essential corrective throughout the curriculum.

  • Philosophical relationship → Rosicrucianism: Waite's histories separate the documentary record of the Rosicrucians from legend. (Waite, The Brotherhood of the Rosy Cross (1924))
Paul Foster Case author · Stage VI

Case developed the Qabalistic Tarot into a disciplined method of contemplation, and his Rosicrucian writings connect the Tarot with the grades of the Rosicrucian path.

  • Philosophical relationship → Rosicrucianism: Case interprets the Rosicrucian grades as stages of interior transformation.
Timothy W. Hogan author · Stage VI

Hogan reads the Masonic degrees through the stages of the alchemical work. In this curriculum his readings are treated as comparative interpretations to be tested against the historical record of both traditions.

  • Comparative symbolic relationship → Freemasonry: Hogan reads the Masonic degrees through the stages of the alchemical work.
  • Comparative symbolic relationship → Alchemy: Hogan's comparison of alchemy and Masonic ritual is treated as a comparative interpretation.

Freemasonry

Freemasonry tradition · Stage I

An initiatory fraternity teaching morality through allegory and symbol, organized in its modern form from the early eighteenth century.

  • Documented historical influence → The Rough Ashlar: The rough and perfect ashlars are emblems of the Craft degrees.
  • Documented historical influence → The Lost Word: The lost word is central to the legend of the third degree.
The Rough Ashlar concept · Stage I

The unworked stone of the Craft, emblem of the imperfect human nature to be shaped.

  • Comparative symbolic relationship → The Philosopher's Stone: Both images describe the rectification of an imperfect nature. A Greco Arcana comparative interpretation.
The Lost Word concept · Stage IX

The word lost with the death of the master builder in the legend of the third degree, whose recovery the Mason seeks.

  • Comparative symbolic relationship → The Philosopher's Stone: Both name a lost or hidden perfection whose recovery restores the seeker. A comparative interpretation.
  • Comparative symbolic relationship → The Holy Grail: The Grail, the Stone and the Lost Word are studied as parallel symbols. A comparative interpretation.
Albert G. Mackey author · Stage I

Mackey explains why Freemasonry teaches through symbol, allegory and the legend of the lost word. His treatment of symbolism is the foundation on which Stage I is built.

  • Documented historical influence → Freemasonry: Mackey was among the most influential nineteenth-century Masonic scholars. (Mackey, The Symbolism of Freemasonry (1869))
Albert Pike author · Stage I

Pike's lectures on the degrees range across Hermeticism, Qabalah, Gnosticism and comparative religion, and substantial portions of <cite>Morals and Dogma</cite> draw on Éliphas Lévi. He demonstrates how Freemasonry was read in the nineteenth century as a doorway into the wider Western mysteries.

  • Documented historical influence → Freemasonry: Pike led the Southern Jurisdiction of the Scottish Rite from 1859 to 1891. (Pike, Morals and Dogma (1871))
Walter Leslie Wilmshurst author · Stage I

Wilmshurst reads the Craft degrees as a map of spiritual regeneration, treating the lodge as an image of the human being. He is the clearest bridge between the moral instruction of the lodge and the interior work of the mysteries.

  • Philosophical relationship → Freemasonry: Wilmshurst interprets the degrees as a map of spiritual regeneration. (Wilmshurst, The Meaning of Masonry (1922))

vi. The Masonic Connection

Documented The legend of the lost word and its substitute is central to the third degree, and the recovery of the word is the subject of the Royal Arch in many Masonic systems. Mackey, Pike and Wilmshurst each devote extended discussion to it.

Greco Arcana interpretation The concluding principle of this curriculum is ours: Freemasonry provides the allegory; the Western Mystery Tradition provides the instruments with which to investigate it; the Great Work is what the student does with that understanding. We present it as the organizing idea of this course of study, not as a doctrine of any Masonic body.

vii. The Work

These exercises develop understanding; they promise no supernatural results. Record each in your study journal.

  1. Journaling

    Return to your first questions

    Reread the list of questions you began in Stage I. Answer each one you now can, noting which stage gave you the answer. Mark the questions that remain open.

  2. Symbolic analysis

    The rough ashlar, again

    Repeat the first exercise of Stage I: read the rough ashlar at four levels. Then compare your two essays. Write a page on what changed.

  3. Comparative investigation

    Concluding work

    Choose one initiatory symbol or allegory: the pillars, the altar, the raising, the Grail or another. Write an essay of at least three thousand words investigating it through at least four of the traditions studied in this curriculum. Distinguish documented history, traditional teaching and your own interpretation throughout.

  4. Discursive meditation

    The return

    Contemplate Figure 23 daily for one week. Trace the circle from Stage I to Stage IX and back. On the last day, write what the Great Work means to you now.

viii. Standard of Completion

Advance because you understand the material, not because you have finished reading it.

Learning objectives

  • Demonstrate an integrated understanding of the Western Mystery Tradition.
  • Articulate how its constituent disciplines illuminate one another.
  • Investigate an initiatory symbol through multiple philosophical and esoteric traditions, distinguishing history, tradition and interpretation.
  • Explain the purpose of initiation as the tradition understands it.

Questions for self-assessment

  1. Can you explain the temple as microcosm using at least three traditions?
  2. What does the comparison of the Grail, the Stone and the Lost Word establish, and what does it not establish?
  3. Which of your first questions remain open, and why?
  4. What does it mean that the symbols have not changed but your understanding of them has?

ix. The Next Threshold

There is no tenth stage. The curriculum returns you to the beginning, to the door, the darkness and the request for light, with the instruments to investigate it for the rest of your life.

The Western Mystery Tradition provides the instruments with which to investigate it.

The Great Work is what the student does with that understanding.
