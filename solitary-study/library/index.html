The Solitary Study

The Recommended Library

45 works of the Western Mystery Tradition in the order the curriculum reads them, each with the reason it belongs, what to investigate while reading it and where to find a legitimate copy.

Public-domain works link to free editions on Project Gutenberg, sacred-texts.com, esotericarchives.com or the Internet Archive. Works in copyright link to a bookseller search; buy from any legitimate seller you prefer. Greco Arcana does not host copies of copyrighted books.

45 works

I. The Gate of Light

Essential reading · Introductory · Public domain

The Symbolism of Freemasonry

Albert G. Mackey

The Symbolism of Freemasonry: Illustrating and Explaining Its Science and Philosophy, Its Legends, Myths and Symbols

First published 1869; revised editions followed

Where to read
Read the opening chapters on the nature of symbolism, then the chapters on the legend of the third degree and the lost word.
Why it belongs here
Mackey explains why Freemasonry teaches through symbol and allegory rather than plain statement. Every later stage assumes the student can read a symbol in this way.
Investigate while reading
How does Mackey distinguish a symbol from an allegory, and why does he believe the ancient mysteries taught by both?
Connections
Wilmshurst takes Mackey's symbolic method inward, reading the same symbols as stages of the soul's development.

Essential reading · Introductory · Public domain

The Meaning of Masonry

Walter Leslie Wilmshurst

First published 1922

Where to read
Begin with the chapters on the deeper symbolism of Masonry and on Masonry as a philosophy.
Why it belongs here
Wilmshurst reads the three Craft degrees as a map of spiritual regeneration. He makes explicit the question this curriculum pursues: what is the candidate actually meant to become?
Investigate while reading
Trace how Wilmshurst interprets the lodge as an image of the human being, and note which of his claims are interpretations rather than historical statements.
Connections
Builds on Mackey's account of symbolism. Pike and Hall extend the same inquiry outward into the wider Western mysteries.

Essential reading · Advanced · Public domain

Morals and Dogma

Albert Pike

Morals and Dogma of the Ancient and Accepted Scottish Rite of Freemasonry

First published 1871

Where to read
Selected readings: the first three chapters (Apprentice, Fellowcraft, Master). Return later to the chapter on the 28th degree, Knight of the Sun, which surveys the Hermetic and Kabbalistic sources Pike drew upon.
Why it belongs here
Pike shows how nineteenth-century Freemasonry was read as a gateway to Hermeticism, Qabalah and comparative religion. He also shows the student how much of that reading depended on Éliphas Lévi.
Investigate while reading
Where does Pike present history, and where does he present interpretation? Mark the passages that point beyond the Craft toward later stages of this curriculum.
Connections
Read after Mackey and Wilmshurst. Pike's debt to Lévi becomes visible in Stage V; his chapter on the Knight of the Sun anticipates Stages III and V.

Essential reading · Introductory · Public domain

The Lost Keys of Freemasonry

Manly P. Hall

The Lost Keys of Freemasonry, or The Secret of Hiram Abiff

First published 1923

Why it belongs here
A short, devotional reading of the Craft as a philosophical inheritance. Hall asks what the builder must do to become the temple, which is the question that sends the student into the rest of the curriculum.
Investigate while reading
Compare Hall's account of Hiram Abiff with the plain legend of the third degree. What has Hall added, and why?
Connections
Read alongside Wilmshurst. Hall's claim that Masonry preserves an older philosophy is tested in Stages II and III.

II. The Nature of Reality

Essential reading · Intermediate · Public domain

Timaeus

Plato

Written c. 360 BCE; translated by Benjamin Jowett (1871)

Where to read
Read Timaeus's account of the making of the cosmos (from 27c). Give particular attention to the World Soul (34b–37c) and the regular solids assigned to the elements (53c–56c).
Why it belongs here
The Timaeus is the single most important source for the Western idea that the visible world is ordered by number, proportion and an invisible pattern.
Investigate while reading
What is the relationship between the eternal model and the visible world? Why does Plato build the elements out of triangles?
Connections
Supplies the philosophy behind the sacred architecture met in Stage I. Plotinus and Proclus systematize the metaphysics; Critchlow returns to the geometry.

Essential reading · Advanced · Public domain

Enneads

Plotinus

The Enneads (selected treatises)

Composed 253–270; edited by Porphyry

Where to read
Selected treatises: I.6 (On Beauty), V.1 (On the Three Primary Hypostases) and VI.9 (On the Good, or the One).
Why it belongs here
Plotinus describes reality as flowing from the One through Intellect and Soul, and the soul's return to its source. That pattern of descent and ascent shapes everything that follows.
Investigate while reading
How can the One be the source of everything without being diminished? What does the soul have to do to return?
Connections
Interprets Plato. Iamblichus and Proclus develop this metaphysics in different directions; Ficino brings it to the Renaissance in Stage III.

On the Mysteries

Iamblichus

On the Mysteries (De mysteriis)

Written c. 300; Thomas Taylor's English translation 1821

Why it belongs here
Iamblichus defends theurgy, divine work performed through ritual, against the purely contemplative path. This is the philosophical root of every later claim that ritual can change the soul.
Investigate while reading
Why does Iamblichus insist that the gods, not human thought, make theurgy effective? How does this differ from Plotinus?
Connections
Responds to the Plotinian tradition through Porphyry. Returns in Stage VII as the philosophical justification of ceremonial practice.

Essential reading · Advanced

The Elements of Theology

Proclus

Written in the 5th century; translated by E. R. Dodds (1933)

Where to read
Read the opening propositions on unity and multiplicity, then the propositions on procession and reversion (25–39).
Why it belongs here
Proclus states the Neoplatonic system as a sequence of propositions, including the triad of remaining, procession and return that Diagram 6 illustrates.
Investigate while reading
Restate propositions 25–39 in your own words. What does it mean for an effect to remain in its cause?
Connections
Systematizes Plotinus and Iamblichus. Underlies the emanation of the Sephiroth studied in Stage V.

The Hidden Geometry of Flowers

Keith Critchlow

The Hidden Geometry of Flowers: Living Rhythms, Form and Number

Floris Books, 2011

Why it belongs here
Critchlow teaches geometry as contemplation: drawing a figure in order to understand the order it expresses. It is the practical companion to the Timaeus.
Investigate while reading
Construct the figures yourself with compass and straightedge before reading the commentary on them.
Connections
Practical companion to the Timaeus. Prepares for the geometrical training in Greer's Way of the Golden Section (Stage VII).

III. The Science of Correspondence

Essential reading · Intermediate · Public domain

Corpus Hermeticum

Anonymous (attributed to Hermes Trismegistus)

The Corpus Hermeticum (the Greek Hermetic treatises)

Greek treatises of the early centuries CE; Latin translation by Marsilio Ficino printed 1471

Where to read
Treatise I (Poimandres), Treatise XI and Treatise XIII (on rebirth). Brian Copenhaver's translation, <cite>Hermetica</cite> (1992), is the standard modern edition.
Why it belongs here
The Hermetic treatises present the cosmos as a living whole in which mind, soul and matter are connected, and they describe the rebirth of the person who comes to know it.
Investigate while reading
How does the Poimandres describe the descent of the soul through the planetary spheres, and what must happen for it to return?
Connections
Shares much with the Platonic metaphysics of Stage II. Ficino's translation launches the Renaissance Hermetic revival.

Asclepius

Anonymous (attributed to Hermes Trismegistus)

Asclepius (the Latin Hermetic dialogue)

Latin text of late antiquity, known throughout the Middle Ages

Where to read
Read with attention to the passages on the human being as a great miracle and on the animating of statues.
Why it belongs here
The Asclepius was the Hermetic text available to medieval readers. Its account of human dignity and of animated images shaped Renaissance ideas about talismans.
Investigate while reading
Why does the Asclepius call the human being a great miracle? What does it say about images that draw down divine powers?
Connections
Companion to the Corpus Hermeticum. Its teaching on images reappears in Ficino and in the talismanic magic of Stage IV.

Three Books on Life

Marsilio Ficino

Three Books on Life (De vita libri tres)

First published 1489; critical edition and translation by Carol V. Kaske and John R. Clark (1989)

Where to read
Concentrate on Book III, On Obtaining Life from the Heavens.
Why it belongs here
Ficino applies Platonic and Hermetic cosmology to the health of the scholar, using music, images, foods and timing to draw on celestial influence. This is the bridge from philosophy to practice.
Investigate while reading
How does Ficino explain the medium through which the heavens act on the body? Which of his practices are medical, which astrological and which magical?
Connections
Applies the Hermetic and Platonic texts he translated. Agrippa systematizes this approach; Warnock teaches its astrological reasoning in Stage IV.

Three Books of Occult Philosophy

Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa

Three Books of Occult Philosophy (De occulta philosophia libri tres)

Draft c. 1510; complete Latin edition 1533; English translation by J. F. 1651

Where to read
Book I (natural magic and the elemental world), Book II (mathematics and the celestial world) and Book III (ceremonial magic and the intellectual world). Books II and III are also at esotericarchives.com.
Why it belongs here
Agrippa is the central junction of this curriculum. His three worlds organize natural magic, astrology, Christian Cabala and ceremonial magic into one structure that later systems inherit.
Investigate while reading
Map each of the three books to the world it describes. How does a power descend from the intellectual world into a stone or a plant?
Connections
Draws on Ficino, Pico, Reuchlin and Albertus. Every later stage returns to Agrippa: Stage IV to Book I, Stages V and VII to Books II and III.

Essential reading · Intermediate

Book of Minerals

Albertus Magnus

Book of Minerals (De mineralibus)

13th century; English translation by Dorothy Wyckoff (Oxford, 1967)

Where to read
Read the discussion of the powers of stones and the alphabetical lapidary.
Why it belongs here
Albertus shows how a medieval natural philosopher reasoned about the hidden powers of stones: carefully, with reference to authorities and experience.
Investigate while reading
How does Albertus account for powers in a stone that its matter alone cannot explain?
Connections
Supplies the natural philosophy behind Agrippa's Book I. Compare his reasoning with the modern references of Cunningham and Illes.

V. The Tree of Life

Essential reading · Intermediate · Public domain

Sepher Yetzirah

Anonymous; translated by W. W. Westcott

Sepher Yetzirah: The Book of Formation (selected introductory material)

Hebrew text of uncertain date, usually placed between the 3rd and 6th centuries; Westcott's translation 1887

Where to read
Read the first chapter, on the ten Sefirot and the thirty-two paths of wisdom.
Why it belongs here
The Sefer Yetzirah describes creation through ten numbers and twenty-two letters. It is the oldest source for the structure the later Tree of Life arranges.
Investigate while reading
What are the ten Sefirot in this text, before the later medieval doctrine of divine attributes developed?
Connections
Westcott's and Mathers's works show how the Golden Dawn read this material.
