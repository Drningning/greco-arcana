Stage III Scientia Hermetica

The Science of Correspondence

How are man, nature and the cosmos connected?

Recommended duration: about 4 months · 4 essential readings · 4 exercises ·

i. The Threshold

A short text, carried from Arabic into Latin in the Middle Ages and attributed to Hermes Trismegistus, begins with a promise of certainty and continues: that which is below is like that which is above, and that which is above is like that which is below, to accomplish the miracles of the one thing. This is the Emerald Tablet, and few sentences in the Western tradition have been quoted more often.

Robert Fludd gave the idea one of its most famous visual forms in 1617. On the title page of his history of the two worlds, a human figure stands inside concentric circles of the elements, planets and stars, so that the structure of the universe and the structure of the person are drawn as one design.

The principle is easy to state and easy to trivialize. Correspondence does not simply mean that one thing reminds us of another. In its traditional form it is a metaphysical proposition: things that appear separate participate in a common principle, and so they are related whether or not anyone notices.

ii. The Purpose

This stage turns the metaphysics of Stage II into the philosophy that organizes nearly all later practice. You will learn to explain correspondence as a philosophical proposition rather than a list of arbitrary associations, and to understand Agrippa's three worlds, the structure through which natural magic, astrology, Qabalah and ceremonial magic are joined.

It is equally important to know what correspondence is not. It is a traditional metaphysical claim, not an empirically established scientific finding. The student who understands the difference can study the tradition seriously without misrepresenting it.

iii. Prerequisites

  • Stage II, The Nature of Reality.
  • A working understanding of emanation and of procession and return.
  • Familiarity with the seven classical planets and four elements is helpful but will be developed further in Stage IV.
  • Before beginning, review Stage II, The Nature of Reality.

iv. The Intellectual Architecture

The Corpus Hermeticum and the Asclepius present a living cosmos in which mind, soul and matter are connected, and in which the human being is a microcosm able to know and even to imitate the divine order. These texts circulated in late antiquity alongside the Platonism of Stage II, and much of their cosmology is Platonic.

Marsilio Ficino translated the Hermetic treatises for Cosimo de' Medici before he finished translating Plato, believing Hermes the more ancient sage. In the third book of De vita he applies this cosmology to the health of the scholar, showing how music, images, scents, foods and the choice of time can draw on celestial influence. Ficino is the bridge from contemplative philosophy to celestial practice.

Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa is the great junction of the curriculum. His Three Books of Occult Philosophy arrange all of this into three worlds: the elemental world of natural things (Book I), the celestial world of stars and number (Book II) and the intellectual world of angels, divine names and religious ceremony (Book III). Powers descend from the higher world to the lower, and the magician ascends by working through them in turn (Figure 9). Agrippa also draws in the Christian Cabala of Pico della Mirandola and Johannes Reuchlin, which is why he stands at the meeting point of so many lines on the map.

Select Agrippa on the map and watch what lights up: Renaissance Hermeticism, Ficino, Christian Cabala, astrology, natural magic and every later ceremonial system that borrowed his tables.

Hermetic & Renaissance philosophy

Corpus Hermeticum text · Stage III

Greek Hermetic treatises of the early centuries CE, translated into Latin by Ficino and printed in 1471.

  • Documented historical influence → Hermeticism: The treatises define the Hermetic philosophy.
  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Marsilio Ficino: Ficino translated the Corpus Hermeticum into Latin for Cosimo de' Medici; it was printed in 1471. (Ficino, Pimander (1471))
Hermeticism tradition · Stage III

The philosophy of the Greek and Latin Hermetic texts: a living cosmos, the human being as microcosm and the rebirth of the knower.

  • Documented historical influence → Macrocosm and microcosm: The doctrine of the human microcosm is central to the Hermetic texts.
  • Documented historical influence → Robert Fludd: Fludd's works are a major expression of seventeenth-century Hermetic philosophy.
Renaissance occult philosophy tradition · Stage III

The synthesis of Platonism, Hermeticism, astrology, natural magic and Christian Cabala in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries.

Marsilio Ficino author · Stage III

Ficino reintroduced Plato, Plotinus and Hermes to the Latin West and joined them in one philosophy. His astrological medicine in the third book of <cite>De vita</cite> is the bridge between Platonic cosmology and practical celestial magic.

  • Documented historical influence → Renaissance occult philosophy: Ficino's translations and De vita are central to Renaissance occult philosophy.
  • Documented historical influence → Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa: Agrippa draws extensively on Ficino's De vita in the first two books of his Occult Philosophy. (Agrippa, De occulta philosophia I–II)
The three worlds concept · Stage III

Agrippa's elemental, celestial and intellectual worlds, the framework that organizes natural magic, astrology and ceremonial magic.

  • Educational prerequisite → Natural magic: Agrippa's elemental world is studied in detail in Stage IV.
Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa author · Stage III

Agrippa organized the magical knowledge of antiquity and the Middle Ages into three worlds: elemental, celestial and intellectual. His synthesis joins natural magic, astrology, Christian Cabala and ceremonial magic, and almost every later Western system draws on his tables.

  • Documented historical influence → The three worlds: Agrippa arranges occult philosophy into elemental, celestial and intellectual worlds. (Agrippa, De occulta philosophia (1533))
  • Documented historical influence → Natural magic: Book I of Agrippa's Occult Philosophy is devoted to natural magic.
Macrocosm and microcosm concept · Stage III

The doctrine that the human being is a small world whose structure corresponds to the structure of the universe.

Robert Fludd figure · Stage III

English physician and Hermetic philosopher (1574–1637) whose illustrated works of 1617 onward gave the macrocosm and microcosm their famous visual form; an early defender of the Rosicrucians.

  • Documented historical influence → Macrocosm and microcosm: Fludd's illustrations of the macrocosm and microcosm (1617 onward) became the classic images of the doctrine.

Astrology, natural & folk magic

Traditional astrology tradition · Stage IV

The traditional science of the seven planets, the zodiac, the houses and the choice of times.

  • Documented historical influence → Marsilio Ficino: Ficino's astrological medicine applies traditional astrology.
Picatrix text · Stage IV

A manual of astrological magic translated from Arabic (Ghāyat al-Ḥakīm) into Spanish and Latin in the thirteenth century; translated into English by Greer and Warnock.

  • Documented historical influence → Marsilio Ficino: Ficino's De vita III draws on the astrological magic of the Picatrix tradition. (Ficino, De vita libri tres III)
Natural magic tradition · Stage IV

The use of the hidden virtues of stones, plants, metals and animals, treated by medieval and Renaissance authors as a branch of natural philosophy.

Kabbalah & Qabalah

Christian Cabala tradition · Stage V

The Renaissance reading of Kabbalistic texts as confirmations of Christian doctrine, begun by Pico della Mirandola and Johannes Reuchlin.

  • Documented historical influence → Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa: Agrippa lectured on Reuchlin's De verbo mirifico at Dole in 1509 and drew Christian Cabala into his third book. (Agrippa, De occulta philosophia III)

v. Essential Reading

Essential reading

Read in the order given.

1 Essential reading · Intermediate · Public domain

Corpus Hermeticum

Anonymous (attributed to Hermes Trismegistus)

The Corpus Hermeticum (the Greek Hermetic treatises)

Greek treatises of the early centuries CE; Latin translation by Marsilio Ficino printed 1471

Where to read
Treatise I (Poimandres), Treatise XI and Treatise XIII (on rebirth). Brian Copenhaver's translation, <cite>Hermetica</cite> (1992), is the standard modern edition.
Why it belongs here
The Hermetic treatises present the cosmos as a living whole in which mind, soul and matter are connected, and they describe the rebirth of the person who comes to know it.
Investigate while reading
How does the Poimandres describe the descent of the soul through the planetary spheres, and what must happen for it to return?
Connections
Shares much with the Platonic metaphysics of Stage II. Ficino's translation launches the Renaissance Hermetic revival.

2 Essential reading · Intermediate · Public domain

Asclepius

Anonymous (attributed to Hermes Trismegistus)

Asclepius (the Latin Hermetic dialogue)

Latin text of late antiquity, known throughout the Middle Ages

Where to read
Read with attention to the passages on the human being as a great miracle and on the animating of statues.
Why it belongs here
The Asclepius was the Hermetic text available to medieval readers. Its account of human dignity and of animated images shaped Renaissance ideas about talismans.
Investigate while reading
Why does the Asclepius call the human being a great miracle? What does it say about images that draw down divine powers?
Connections
Companion to the Corpus Hermeticum. Its teaching on images reappears in Ficino and in the talismanic magic of Stage IV.

3 Essential reading · Advanced

Three Books on Life

Marsilio Ficino

Three Books on Life (De vita libri tres)

First published 1489; critical edition and translation by Carol V. Kaske and John R. Clark (1989)

Where to read
Concentrate on Book III, On Obtaining Life from the Heavens.
Why it belongs here
Ficino applies Platonic and Hermetic cosmology to the health of the scholar, using music, images, foods and timing to draw on celestial influence. This is the bridge from philosophy to practice.
Investigate while reading
How does Ficino explain the medium through which the heavens act on the body? Which of his practices are medical, which astrological and which magical?
Connections
Applies the Hermetic and Platonic texts he translated. Agrippa systematizes this approach; Warnock teaches its astrological reasoning in Stage IV.

4 Essential reading · Advanced · Public domain

Three Books of Occult Philosophy

Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa

Three Books of Occult Philosophy (De occulta philosophia libri tres)

Draft c. 1510; complete Latin edition 1533; English translation by J. F. 1651

Where to read
Book I (natural magic and the elemental world), Book II (mathematics and the celestial world) and Book III (ceremonial magic and the intellectual world). Books II and III are also at esotericarchives.com.
Why it belongs here
Agrippa is the central junction of this curriculum. His three worlds organize natural magic, astrology, Christian Cabala and ceremonial magic into one structure that later systems inherit.
Investigate while reading
Map each of the three books to the world it describes. How does a power descend from the intellectual world into a stone or a plant?
Connections
Draws on Ficino, Pico, Reuchlin and Albertus. Every later stage returns to Agrippa: Stage IV to Book I, Stages V and VII to Books II and III.

vi. The Masonic Connection

Documented Hermetic and Kabbalistic themes appear in the higher degrees of some Masonic rites. Pike's lecture for the twenty-eighth degree of the Scottish Rite, Knight of the Sun, surveys Hermetic and Kabbalistic doctrine at length.

Greco Arcana interpretation The lodge is commonly described as a symbol of the world, and many Masonic writers have read it as a symbol of the human being. The Hermetic doctrine of macrocosm and microcosm unites these two readings: if the person is a small world, then a lodge that images the world also images the person. We offer this as a comparative interpretation that illuminates the Craft, not as evidence that the Craft derives from Hermeticism.

vii. The Work

These exercises develop understanding; they promise no supernatural results. Record each in your study journal.

  1. Symbolic analysis

    Draw the macrocosm and microcosm

    Study Fludd's title page and Figure 8. Draw your own version with the planets in their traditional order, and list one human faculty you would place beside each.

  2. Comparative reading

    Map Agrippa's three books

    Read the opening chapters of each of Agrippa's three books. Make a table with three columns, one for each world, and list the subjects Agrippa assigns to each.

  3. Philosophical contemplation

    Correspondence or association?

    Write a page distinguishing a correspondence in Agrippa's sense from a mere association. Then write a paragraph on why neither is an empirically established scientific claim, and why the tradition remains worth studying.

  4. Observation of traditional correspondences

    Observation of the hours

    For one week, note the time and quality of your best concentration each day. Do not interpret the results astrologically yet; simply observe. You will revisit these notes in Stage IV.

viii. Standard of Completion

Advance because you understand the material, not because you have finished reading it.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the principle of correspondence as a metaphysical proposition.
  • Distinguish that proposition from empirically established scientific claims.
  • Describe Agrippa's three worlds and the content of his three books.
  • Explain Ficino's role in transmitting the Hermetic and Platonic texts to the Renaissance.
  • Explain the relevance of the three worlds to natural magic, astrology, Qabalah and ceremonial magic.

Questions for self-assessment

  1. Can you explain the three worlds and give one example of a power descending through them?
  2. Why did Ficino translate Hermes before finishing Plato?
  3. What does the Asclepius teach about images?
  4. Why is a correspondence more than a resemblance?

ix. The Next Threshold

Agrippa's first book is devoted to the elemental world: the hidden virtues of stones, plants, metals and animals, and the times at which they are strongest. Before the student can understand the ceremonial magic of the third book, the natural philosophy of the first must be learned. The next stage descends into nature.
