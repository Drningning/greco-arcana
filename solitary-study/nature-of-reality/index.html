Stage II Philosophia

The Nature of Reality

What is the relationship between visible and invisible reality?

Recommended duration: about 6 months · 5 essential readings · 4 exercises ·

i. The Threshold

Imagine people who have lived all their lives chained in a cave, facing a wall. Behind them a fire burns, and between the fire and their backs objects are carried to and fro. All the prisoners have ever seen are the shadows those objects cast. They give the shadows names and become expert at predicting which will come next. One of them is released, turned around and dragged up toward the light. At first he is blinded. Only slowly can he look at the objects themselves, and at last at the sun that makes all seeing possible.

The allegory of the cave, from the seventh book of Plato's Republic, is the founding image of Western philosophy's claim that the visible world is not the whole of reality. What we perceive is real, but derivative: an image of an intelligible order that the mind can come to know.

The prisoner's ascent is also the oldest description in Western literature of what the mystery traditions would call initiation: a turning around, a painful adjustment to greater light, and a return to the cave to help those still chained.

ii. The Purpose

This is the longest philosophical stage of the curriculum, and the most important. Its purpose is to give you the metaphysics that every later stage assumes. Hermetic correspondence, the emanation of the Sephiroth, the theurgy of the Golden Dawn and the symbolism of the Masonic temple all rest on the propositions you study here: that the visible world participates in an invisible order, that this order proceeds from a single source, and that the soul can return to that source.

You will also meet the earliest serious argument that ritual can change the soul. Iamblichus's defense of theurgy is the philosophical root of ceremonial magic.

iii. Prerequisites

  • Stage I, The Gate of Light.
  • The ability to distinguish literal, moral, philosophical and esoteric readings of a symbol.
  • Patience: these are demanding texts. Read slowly, and expect to reread.
  • Before beginning, review Stage I, The Gate of Light.

iv. The Intellectual Architecture

Plato establishes the frame: eternal Forms, a world that images them, and in the Timaeus a cosmos ordered by a divine craftsman through number and proportion. Plotinus, six centuries later, systematizes this into a metaphysics of emanation. Reality flows from the One through Intellect (Nous) and Soul into the material world, and the soul's task is to return (Figure 5).

Iamblichus and Proclus take Plotinus in a crucial direction. Iamblichus insists that the descended soul cannot rise by contemplation alone; it needs theurgy, ritual acts established by the gods. Proclus states the whole system as a chain of propositions, including the triad that underlies all later esoteric cosmology: every effect remains in its cause, proceeds from it and returns to it (Figure 6).

Critchlow brings the Platonic emphasis on number back down to the drawing board. The five regular solids of the Timaeus (Figure 7) and the proportions of living forms are studied not as decoration but as evidence of the order Plato described.

Notice the difference between the two views of the map. Historically, these authors stand at the source of much of the Western tradition. Educationally, they come second: the Craft first raises the questions, and the philosophers answer them.

Philosophy & theurgy

Platonism tradition · Stage II

The philosophy of Plato and his successors: eternal Forms, a world that images them and a cosmos ordered by number.

  • Documented historical influence → Plotinus: Plotinus understood himself as an interpreter of Plato. (Plotinus, Enneads)
Plato author · Stage II

Plato's theory of Forms and the cosmology of the <cite>Timaeus</cite>, in which a divine craftsman orders the world by number and proportion, are the deepest root of the Western idea that the visible world images an invisible order.

  • Documented historical influence → Platonism: Plato's dialogues are the foundation of the Platonic tradition. (Plato, Republic, Timaeus)
  • Philosophical relationship → Sacred geometry: The Timaeus builds the elements from regular solids and orders the cosmos by proportion. (Plato, Timaeus 53c–56c)
  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Marsilio Ficino: Ficino translated the complete works of Plato into Latin, printed in 1484.
Neoplatonism tradition · Stage II

The late antique development of Platonism by Plotinus and his successors: emanation from the One and the return of the soul.

Plotinus author · Stage II

Plotinus describes reality as proceeding from the One through Intellect and Soul and returning to its source. That pattern of procession and return underlies Renaissance Hermeticism, Christian mysticism and the initiatory ascent of the later mystery schools.

  • Documented historical influence → Neoplatonism: Plotinus's Enneads are the founding texts of Neoplatonism. (Enneads, ed. Porphyry)
  • Documented historical influence → Iamblichus: Iamblichus studied in the circle of Porphyry, Plotinus's student and editor, and developed theurgy in debate with that school. (Iamblichus, On the Mysteries)
  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Marsilio Ficino: Ficino translated the Enneads into Latin, printed in 1492.
Theurgy concept · Stage II

Divine work: ritual acts through which, according to Iamblichus, the soul is joined to the gods. The philosophical root of ceremonial magic.

Iamblichus author · Stage II

Iamblichus argued that the soul is restored to the divine not by thought alone but through ritual acts that the gods themselves established. That argument is the philosophical root of theurgy in the Western tradition.

  • Documented historical influence → Theurgy: Iamblichus's On the Mysteries is the classic defense of theurgy. (Iamblichus, On the Mysteries)
  • Documented historical influence → Proclus: Proclus inherited and systematized Iamblichean Neoplatonism, including theurgy. (Proclus, Elements of Theology; Platonic Theology)
  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Marsilio Ficino: Ficino made a Latin paraphrase of On the Mysteries, printed in 1497.
Procession and return concept · Stage II

The Neoplatonic triad of remaining, procession and return: every effect remains in its cause, proceeds from it and returns to it.

Proclus author · Stage II

Proclus gave Neoplatonism its most systematic form, setting out procession, remaining and return as a sequence of propositions. Through Christian and Arabic intermediaries his metaphysics reached the medieval and Renaissance thinkers of Stages III and V.

  • Documented historical influence → Procession and return: Proclus formulates the triad of remaining, procession and return. (Proclus, Elements of Theology, props. 25–39)
Sacred geometry tradition · Stage II

The study of geometrical form and proportion as an expression of cosmic order, rooted in the Platonic and Pythagorean traditions.

Keith Critchlow author · Stage II

Critchlow teaches geometry as a contemplative discipline in the Platonic tradition, showing how number and proportion appear in nature and in sacred architecture.

  • Philosophical relationship → Sacred geometry: Critchlow teaches geometry as a contemplative discipline in the Platonic tradition.

Hermetic & Renaissance philosophy

Marsilio Ficino author · Stage III

Ficino reintroduced Plato, Plotinus and Hermes to the Latin West and joined them in one philosophy. His astrological medicine in the third book of <cite>De vita</cite> is the bridge between Platonic cosmology and practical celestial magic.

v. Essential Reading

Essential reading

Read in the order given.

1 Essential reading · Intermediate · Public domain

Timaeus

Plato

Written c. 360 BCE; translated by Benjamin Jowett (1871)

Where to read
Read Timaeus's account of the making of the cosmos (from 27c). Give particular attention to the World Soul (34b–37c) and the regular solids assigned to the elements (53c–56c).
Why it belongs here
The Timaeus is the single most important source for the Western idea that the visible world is ordered by number, proportion and an invisible pattern.
Investigate while reading
What is the relationship between the eternal model and the visible world? Why does Plato build the elements out of triangles?
Connections
Supplies the philosophy behind the sacred architecture met in Stage I. Plotinus and Proclus systematize the metaphysics; Critchlow returns to the geometry.

2 Essential reading · Advanced · Public domain

Enneads

Plotinus

The Enneads (selected treatises)

Composed 253–270; edited by Porphyry

Where to read
Selected treatises: I.6 (On Beauty), V.1 (On the Three Primary Hypostases) and VI.9 (On the Good, or the One).
Why it belongs here
Plotinus describes reality as flowing from the One through Intellect and Soul, and the soul's return to its source. That pattern of descent and ascent shapes everything that follows.
Investigate while reading
How can the One be the source of everything without being diminished? What does the soul have to do to return?
Connections
Interprets Plato. Iamblichus and Proclus develop this metaphysics in different directions; Ficino brings it to the Renaissance in Stage III.

On the Mysteries

Iamblichus

On the Mysteries (De mysteriis)

Written c. 300; Thomas Taylor's English translation 1821

Why it belongs here
Iamblichus defends theurgy, divine work performed through ritual, against the purely contemplative path. This is the philosophical root of every later claim that ritual can change the soul.
Investigate while reading
Why does Iamblichus insist that the gods, not human thought, make theurgy effective? How does this differ from Plotinus?
Connections
Responds to the Plotinian tradition through Porphyry. Returns in Stage VII as the philosophical justification of ceremonial practice.

vi. The Masonic Connection

Documented Geometry holds a special place in Masonic lectures, and the forty-seventh proposition of Euclid's first book appears among the emblems of the Master Mason's degree in many jurisdictions. The Fellowcraft's winding staircase is traditionally associated with the liberal arts, including geometry and astronomy.

Greco Arcana interpretation The Platonic tradition supplies a reason why geometry should be honored in a lodge at all. If the cosmos is ordered by number and proportion, then the study of geometry is a study of that order, and the building of a temple according to true proportion becomes an act of participation in it. We present this as a philosophical reading of the Craft's emphasis on geometry, not a claim about the historical intentions of its framers.

vii. The Work

These exercises develop understanding; they promise no supernatural results. Record each in your study journal.

  1. Philosophical contemplation

    The cave in your own life

    Read the allegory of the cave in full. Then write about one area in which you once mistook a shadow for the thing itself. What turned you around?

  2. Geometrical investigation

    Construct the solids

    Using paper nets, build the five regular solids. Count the faces, edges and vertices of each, and record which element the Timaeus assigns to each. Why is the dodecahedron set apart?

  3. Discursive meditation

    Procession and return

    Contemplate Proclus's triad of remaining, procession and return. Find an example in nature, in a craft and in your own experience. Draw the triad as a diagram in your notebook.

  4. Geometrical investigation

    Draw the vesica

    With compass and straightedge alone, construct the vesica piscis, an equilateral triangle, a hexagon and a pentagon. Write a paragraph on what you learned from doing it rather than reading about it.

viii. Standard of Completion

Advance because you understand the material, not because you have finished reading it.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the theory of Forms and the allegory of the cave.
  • Describe the procession from the One through Intellect and Soul, and the soul's return.
  • Explain why Iamblichus believed theurgy necessary and how he differs from Plotinus.
  • State Proclus's triad of remaining, procession and return in your own words.
  • Explain how a material object or ritual action might participate in an intelligible principle within these systems.

Questions for self-assessment

  1. Could you explain to a friend why Plato thought the visible world an image?
  2. What does it mean for an effect to remain in its cause?
  3. Why does the Timaeus build the elements from triangles?
  4. On Iamblichus's view, who makes a theurgic rite effective?

ix. The Next Threshold

If the cosmos is a single ordered whole proceeding from one source, then its parts must be related to one another and to the human being who perceives them. Late antique and Renaissance thinkers developed that conclusion into a science of correspondences. The next stage follows the Hermetic texts, Ficino and Agrippa as they turn this metaphysics into a philosophy of the connected cosmos.
