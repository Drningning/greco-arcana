Stage I Porta Lucis

The Gate of Light

Why are the mysteries communicated through initiation, allegory and symbolism?

Recommended duration: about 3 months · 4 essential readings · 4 exercises ·

i. The Threshold

A candidate stands at a door he cannot see through. He has been prepared in a way he does not understand, and when he is admitted he is asked, in one form or another, what he most desires. The traditional answer is a single word: light.

Nothing in that exchange is secret in any meaningful sense. Its outline has been published for more than two centuries. Yet the candidate who has passed through it knows something that the reader of the published account does not, because he has undergone the symbol rather than read about it.

That is the first problem of the Western mysteries, and the reason this curriculum begins with Freemasonry. Some kinds of understanding cannot be handed over as information. They have to be approached through images, enacted in ceremony and slowly interpreted. A symbol says more than its author can state, and it keeps saying more as the person contemplating it changes.

ii. The Purpose

This stage teaches you to read a symbol properly. By the end of it you should be able to take any emblem of the Craft and distinguish four levels at which it can be understood: the literal, the moral, the philosophical and the esoteric (Figure 4).

It also asks a question that the rest of the curriculum answers. The Craft presents a temple, a murdered architect, a lost word and a promise of light. Where did these images come from, and what were they meant to accomplish? Freemasonry does not answer those questions within its own lectures. It points beyond itself, to the philosophers, Hermeticists, Qabalists and Rosicrucians whose ideas it carries in symbolic form.

iii. Prerequisites

  • No prior study is required. This is the first stage.
  • Masonic membership is not required. Everything discussed here is drawn from published sources.
  • A notebook reserved for this course, used from the first day.

iv. The Intellectual Architecture

The four essential readings form a deliberate sequence. Mackey supplies the method: what a symbol is, how an allegory differs from it and why the ancient mysteries used both. Wilmshurst turns that method inward, reading the three degrees as stages in the regeneration of the soul. Pike shows how far nineteenth-century Freemasons carried the inquiry, into Hermeticism, Qabalah and the comparative study of religion, and reveals in passing how much he owed to Éliphas Lévi. Hall closes the stage by asking what the builder must become in order to complete the temple.

The authors do not agree with one another, and you should not try to make them agree. Mackey is a historian and jurist who writes with restraint. Wilmshurst is a contemplative. Pike is encyclopedic and speculative. Hall is devotional. Learning to hold their differences without collapsing them is itself part of the work.

Look at the map for this stage and notice where the lines lead. Freemasonry sits at the beginning of the educational path, but its historical relationships run outward in several directions: to the Platonic idea of the cosmos as an ordered building, to Hermetic and Rosicrucian currents of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and forward to the Golden Dawn, whose founders were Freemasons.

Philosophy & theurgy

Platonism tradition · Stage II

The philosophy of Plato and his successors: eternal Forms, a world that images them and a cosmos ordered by number.

Kabbalah & Qabalah

Éliphas Lévi author · Stage V

Lévi is the hinge between Renaissance occult philosophy and the modern occult revival. He presented magic as a coherent philosophy, systematized the doctrine of the Astral Light, and connected the twenty-two Tarot trumps with the twenty-two letters of the Hebrew alphabet. That connection, revised by the Golden Dawn, is the foundation of Hermetic Tarot.

  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Albert Pike: Substantial portions of Morals and Dogma draw on Lévi's writings. (Pike, Morals and Dogma)
  • Documented historical influence → The Golden Dawn: The Golden Dawn developed its attributions of the Tarot to the Hebrew letters from Lévi, with revisions.

Alchemy & Rosicrucianism

Rosicrucianism tradition · Stage VI

The current launched by the manifestos of 1614–1616: a hidden fraternity, the vault of Christian Rosenkreutz and the regeneration of humanity.

  • Comparative symbolic relationship → Freemasonry: Rosicrucian themes appear in several Masonic degrees; the historical relationship between the manifestos and early speculative Freemasonry is uncertain.
  • Textual transmission or adaptation → The Golden Dawn: The Golden Dawn's Adeptus Minor grade enacts the opening of the vault of Christian Rosenkreutz.

Freemasonry

Freemasonry tradition · Stage I

An initiatory fraternity teaching morality through allegory and symbol, organized in its modern form from the early eighteenth century.

  • Documented historical influence → The Rough Ashlar: The rough and perfect ashlars are emblems of the Craft degrees.
  • Documented historical influence → The Lost Word: The lost word is central to the legend of the third degree.
  • Documented historical influence → The Golden Dawn: The Golden Dawn's founders were Freemasons and members of the Societas Rosicruciana in Anglia, which admitted only Master Masons.
  • Educational prerequisite → Platonism: The Craft raises questions about order, light and the temple that Platonic philosophy addresses (Stage I to II).
The Rough Ashlar concept · Stage I

The unworked stone of the Craft, emblem of the imperfect human nature to be shaped.

The Lost Word concept · Stage IX

The word lost with the death of the master builder in the legend of the third degree, whose recovery the Mason seeks.

Albert G. Mackey author · Stage I

Mackey explains why Freemasonry teaches through symbol, allegory and the legend of the lost word. His treatment of symbolism is the foundation on which Stage I is built.

  • Documented historical influence → Freemasonry: Mackey was among the most influential nineteenth-century Masonic scholars. (Mackey, The Symbolism of Freemasonry (1869))
Albert Pike author · Stage I

Pike's lectures on the degrees range across Hermeticism, Qabalah, Gnosticism and comparative religion, and substantial portions of <cite>Morals and Dogma</cite> draw on Éliphas Lévi. He demonstrates how Freemasonry was read in the nineteenth century as a doorway into the wider Western mysteries.

  • Documented historical influence → Freemasonry: Pike led the Southern Jurisdiction of the Scottish Rite from 1859 to 1891. (Pike, Morals and Dogma (1871))
Walter Leslie Wilmshurst author · Stage I

Wilmshurst reads the Craft degrees as a map of spiritual regeneration, treating the lodge as an image of the human being. He is the clearest bridge between the moral instruction of the lodge and the interior work of the mysteries.

  • Philosophical relationship → Freemasonry: Wilmshurst interprets the degrees as a map of spiritual regeneration. (Wilmshurst, The Meaning of Masonry (1922))
Manly P. Hall author · Stage I

Hall introduced generations of readers to the symbolic unity of the Western traditions. His early book on Freemasonry treats the Craft as the custodian of a philosophical inheritance, which is exactly the question Stage I asks the student to examine.

  • Philosophical relationship → Freemasonry: Hall reads the Craft as the custodian of a philosophical inheritance. (Hall, The Lost Keys of Freemasonry (1923))

Ceremonial magic

The Golden Dawn tradition · Stage VII

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, founded in London in 1888, which synthesized the Qabalah, astrology, Enochian magic, Tarot and Rosicrucian symbolism.

Connections
Wilmshurst takes Mackey's symbolic method inward, reading the same symbols as stages of the soul's development.

vi. The Masonic Connection

Documented The Craft degrees teach by allegory and illustrate with symbols. They present King Solomon's Temple as their setting, the working tools as instruments of moral instruction, and the legend of the third degree as the narrative of a loss. The Fellowcraft lecture in its historical forms commends the seven liberal arts and sciences, giving geometry particular honor. These features are described in the published monitors and in the works of Mackey and Pike.

Greco Arcana interpretation We read the Craft as an initiatory allegory whose questions cannot be answered from inside it. The temple invites the study of sacred architecture and cosmology; the light invites the study of illumination in Platonic and Hermetic philosophy; the lost word invites the study of the divine names and of regeneration. The rest of this curriculum follows those invitations. It does not claim that Freemasonry is the historical origin of these traditions.

vii. The Work

These exercises develop understanding; they promise no supernatural results. Record each in your study journal.

  1. Symbolic analysis

    The four readings of a symbol

    Choose one emblem: the rough ashlar, the twenty-four-inch gauge or the square. Write a page on each of its four levels: what it is literally, what it teaches morally, what philosophical idea it expresses, and what interior transformation it might signify. Use Figure 4 as your template.

  2. Discursive meditation

    Discursive meditation on light

    Sit for fifteen minutes each day for one week with a single sentence: "What is the light I am asking for?" Do not try to answer it at once. At the end of each session write down whatever arose, without editing.

  3. Comparative reading

    Comparative reading

    Read what Mackey and Wilmshurst each say about the legend of the third degree. Write a short comparison: where do they agree, where do they differ, and which claims are historical and which interpretive?

  4. Journaling

    Begin the study journal

    Open the notebook you will keep for the whole curriculum. Record the date, the reading, a single passage that struck you and a question you cannot yet answer. Keep the question list: you will return to it in Stage IX.

viii. Standard of Completion

Advance because you understand the material, not because you have finished reading it.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish literal, moral, philosophical and esoteric interpretations of a symbol.
  • Explain the difference between a symbol and an allegory, with an example of each from the Craft.
  • Summarize the different approaches of Mackey, Wilmshurst, Pike and Hall.
  • Identify at least three questions raised by the Craft that its own lectures do not answer.

Questions for self-assessment

  1. Can you explain the rough ashlar at all four levels without notes?
  2. Can you say which of Wilmshurst's claims are historical and which are interpretations?
  3. Why does a symbol continue to reveal more as the person contemplating it changes?
  4. Why does Freemasonry lead outward to other traditions rather than containing them?

ix. The Next Threshold

Freemasonry presents its temple as an image of an ordered cosmos and of the human being. But what is the order it images? Why should a building, a geometrical figure or a ritual action be able to participate in something invisible? Those are philosophical questions, and the philosophy that first answered them is Plato's. The next stage turns to the nature of reality itself.
