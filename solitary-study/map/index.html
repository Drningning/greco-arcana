The Intellectual Relationship Map

Traditions, authors, texts and concepts of the Western Mystery Tradition, and the 129 relationships between them. Two views: the educational path, in the order this curriculum teaches them, and the historical network, in the order they actually developed. The two are deliberately different.

The interactive map requires JavaScript. The complete map is described in words below.

Select any tradition, author, text or concept to see its explanation, its influences, the developments that followed and the stages in which it is studied.

How to read the map

Every line is classified as one of six kinds of relationship, and every line carries a short explanation of why it exists. A documented historical influence means that the record shows one party reading, translating or learning from the other. A comparative symbolic relationship means only that a parallel can usefully be drawn; it makes no claim of transmission. Where a relationship is uncertain the explanation says so.

Select a node to see what fed into it and what developed from it. Ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn and Hermetic Tarot are deliberately points of convergence: select them to see how many earlier currents they draw upon.

Educational order is not historical order. Freemasonry stands at the beginning of the educational path because its allegories pose the questions the curriculum pursues, not because the older traditions descend from it. They do not.

Philosophy & theurgy

Platonism tradition · Stage II

The philosophy of Plato and his successors: eternal Forms, a world that images them and a cosmos ordered by number.

  • Documented historical influence → Plotinus: Plotinus understood himself as an interpreter of Plato. (Plotinus, Enneads)
  • Philosophical relationship → Hermeticism: Much of the cosmology of the Hermetic treatises is Platonic in character.
Plato author · Stage II

Plato's theory of Forms and the cosmology of the <cite>Timaeus</cite>, in which a divine craftsman orders the world by number and proportion, are the deepest root of the Western idea that the visible world images an invisible order.

  • Documented historical influence → Platonism: Plato's dialogues are the foundation of the Platonic tradition. (Plato, Republic, Timaeus)
  • Philosophical relationship → Sacred geometry: The Timaeus builds the elements from regular solids and orders the cosmos by proportion. (Plato, Timaeus 53c–56c)
  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Marsilio Ficino: Ficino translated the complete works of Plato into Latin, printed in 1484.
Neoplatonism tradition · Stage II

The late antique development of Platonism by Plotinus and his successors: emanation from the One and the return of the soul.

  • Documented historical influence → Renaissance occult philosophy: Renaissance occult philosophy is built on the Neoplatonic texts Ficino translated.
  • Educational prerequisite → Hermeticism: The metaphysics of Stage II is the foundation of Hermetic correspondence (Stage II to III).
Plotinus author · Stage II

Plotinus describes reality as proceeding from the One through Intellect and Soul and returning to its source. That pattern of procession and return underlies Renaissance Hermeticism, Christian mysticism and the initiatory ascent of the later mystery schools.

  • Documented historical influence → Neoplatonism: Plotinus's Enneads are the founding texts of Neoplatonism. (Enneads, ed. Porphyry)
  • Documented historical influence → Iamblichus: Iamblichus studied in the circle of Porphyry, Plotinus's student and editor, and developed theurgy in debate with that school. (Iamblichus, On the Mysteries)
  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Marsilio Ficino: Ficino translated the Enneads into Latin, printed in 1492.
Theurgy concept · Stage II

Divine work: ritual acts through which, according to Iamblichus, the soul is joined to the gods. The philosophical root of ceremonial magic.

  • Philosophical relationship → Ceremonial magic: Theurgy supplies the philosophical claim that ritual can transform the soul, on which later ceremonial magic relies.
Iamblichus author · Stage II

Iamblichus argued that the soul is restored to the divine not by thought alone but through ritual acts that the gods themselves established. That argument is the philosophical root of theurgy in the Western tradition.

  • Documented historical influence → Theurgy: Iamblichus's On the Mysteries is the classic defense of theurgy. (Iamblichus, On the Mysteries)
  • Documented historical influence → Proclus: Proclus inherited and systematized Iamblichean Neoplatonism, including theurgy. (Proclus, Elements of Theology; Platonic Theology)
  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Marsilio Ficino: Ficino made a Latin paraphrase of On the Mysteries, printed in 1497.
Procession and return concept · Stage II

The Neoplatonic triad of remaining, procession and return: every effect remains in its cause, proceeds from it and returns to it.

  • Philosophical relationship → The Tree of Life: The descent and return on the Tree parallel the Neoplatonic procession and return.
Proclus author · Stage II

Proclus gave Neoplatonism its most systematic form, setting out procession, remaining and return as a sequence of propositions. Through Christian and Arabic intermediaries his metaphysics reached the medieval and Renaissance thinkers of Stages III and V.

  • Documented historical influence → Procession and return: Proclus formulates the triad of remaining, procession and return. (Proclus, Elements of Theology, props. 25–39)
Sacred geometry tradition · Stage II

The study of geometrical form and proportion as an expression of cosmic order, rooted in the Platonic and Pythagorean traditions.

  • Comparative symbolic relationship → Freemasonry: Masonic lectures honor geometry; the Platonic tradition explains why geometry might image cosmic order.
Keith Critchlow author · Stage II

Critchlow teaches geometry as a contemplative discipline in the Platonic tradition, showing how number and proportion appear in nature and in sacred architecture.

  • Philosophical relationship → Sacred geometry: Critchlow teaches geometry as a contemplative discipline in the Platonic tradition.

Hermetic & Renaissance philosophy

Corpus Hermeticum text · Stage III

Greek Hermetic treatises of the early centuries CE, translated into Latin by Ficino and printed in 1471.

  • Documented historical influence → Hermeticism: The treatises define the Hermetic philosophy.
  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Marsilio Ficino: Ficino translated the Corpus Hermeticum into Latin for Cosimo de' Medici; it was printed in 1471. (Ficino, Pimander (1471))
Hermeticism tradition · Stage III

The philosophy of the Greek and Latin Hermetic texts: a living cosmos, the human being as microcosm and the rebirth of the knower.

  • Documented historical influence → Macrocosm and microcosm: The doctrine of the human microcosm is central to the Hermetic texts.
  • Documented historical influence → Robert Fludd: Fludd's works are a major expression of seventeenth-century Hermetic philosophy.
Renaissance occult philosophy tradition · Stage III

The synthesis of Platonism, Hermeticism, astrology, natural magic and Christian Cabala in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries.

Marsilio Ficino author · Stage III

Ficino reintroduced Plato, Plotinus and Hermes to the Latin West and joined them in one philosophy. His astrological medicine in the third book of <cite>De vita</cite> is the bridge between Platonic cosmology and practical celestial magic.

  • Documented historical influence → Renaissance occult philosophy: Ficino's translations and De vita are central to Renaissance occult philosophy.
  • Documented historical influence → Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa: Agrippa draws extensively on Ficino's De vita in the first two books of his Occult Philosophy. (Agrippa, De occulta philosophia I–II)
The three worlds concept · Stage III

Agrippa's elemental, celestial and intellectual worlds, the framework that organizes natural magic, astrology and ceremonial magic.

  • Educational prerequisite → Talismanic magic: The three worlds explain how a celestial virtue can be drawn into a material object.
  • Educational prerequisite → Ceremonial magic: Agrippa's third book, on the intellectual world, is the theory of ceremonial magic.
  • Educational prerequisite → Natural magic: Agrippa's elemental world is studied in detail in Stage IV.
Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa author · Stage III

Agrippa organized the magical knowledge of antiquity and the Middle Ages into three worlds: elemental, celestial and intellectual. His synthesis joins natural magic, astrology, Christian Cabala and ceremonial magic, and almost every later Western system draws on his tables.

  • Documented historical influence → The three worlds: Agrippa arranges occult philosophy into elemental, celestial and intellectual worlds. (Agrippa, De occulta philosophia (1533))
  • Documented historical influence → Natural magic: Book I of Agrippa's Occult Philosophy is devoted to natural magic.
  • Documented historical influence → John Dee: Dee owned and studied Agrippa's Occult Philosophy.
  • Textual transmission or adaptation → The Golden Dawn: Golden Dawn teaching draws on Agrippa's tables of correspondence.
Macrocosm and microcosm concept · Stage III

The doctrine that the human being is a small world whose structure corresponds to the structure of the universe.

  • Philosophical relationship → Lesser Ritual of the Pentagram: The pentagram as an image of the human being expresses the microcosm doctrine.
Robert Fludd figure · Stage III

English physician and Hermetic philosopher (1574–1637) whose illustrated works of 1617 onward gave the macrocosm and microcosm their famous visual form; an early defender of the Rosicrucians.

  • Documented historical influence → Macrocosm and microcosm: Fludd's illustrations of the macrocosm and microcosm (1617 onward) became the classic images of the doctrine.
  • Documented historical influence → Rosicrucianism: Fludd defended the Rosicrucians in print from 1616.

Astrology, natural & folk magic

Traditional astrology tradition · Stage IV

The traditional science of the seven planets, the zodiac, the houses and the choice of times.

  • Documented historical influence → Marsilio Ficino: Ficino's astrological medicine applies traditional astrology.
  • Practical application → Talismanic magic: Astrology selects the time and planetary image of a talisman.
  • Practical application → The Golden Dawn: Astrological correspondences are integral to Golden Dawn ritual and teaching.
  • Practical application → Hermetic Tarot: The minor arcana and trumps carry astrological attributions in Hermetic Tarot.
  • Educational prerequisite → The Tree of Life: The planets must be known before they can be placed on the Tree (Stage IV to V).
Picatrix text · Stage IV

A manual of astrological magic translated from Arabic (Ghāyat al-Ḥakīm) into Spanish and Latin in the thirteenth century; translated into English by Greer and Warnock.

  • Documented historical influence → Marsilio Ficino: Ficino's De vita III draws on the astrological magic of the Picatrix tradition. (Ficino, De vita libri tres III)
Natural magic tradition · Stage IV

The use of the hidden virtues of stones, plants, metals and animals, treated by medieval and Renaissance authors as a branch of natural philosophy.

  • Practical application → Talismanic magic: Natural magic selects the material of a talisman.
  • Comparative symbolic relationship → Folk and traditional magic: Folk practice and learned natural magic share a logic of correspondence; this does not establish a common origin.
  • Practical application → Ceremonial magic: Ceremonial magic uses the materials and correspondences of natural magic.
Albertus Magnus author · Stage IV

Albertus treated the hidden powers of stones, plants and animals as a subject of natural philosophy. Agrippa cites him, and several later books of secrets circulated under his name.

  • Documented historical influence → Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa: Agrippa cites Albertus on the virtues of natural things. (Agrippa, De occulta philosophia I)
  • Documented historical influence → Natural magic: Albertus treated the virtues of stones and plants as natural philosophy. (Albertus Magnus, De mineralibus)
Talismanic magic concept · Stage IV

The making of images and objects at chosen times to draw on celestial influence, where cosmology, astrology, natural magic and symbolism meet.

Folk and traditional magic tradition · Stage IV

Regional and popular magical practice, with its own logic of place, season and material; not to be assumed to descend from learned magic.

Scott Cunningham author · Stage IV

Cunningham's encyclopedia gathers traditional associations of plants for practical use. It is read here as a reference to contemporary practice, to be compared with the older natural philosophy of Albertus and Agrippa.

  • Practical application → Natural magic: Cunningham's encyclopedia applies traditional plant associations in contemporary practice.
Judika Illes author · Stage IV

Illes documents folk and practical magic from many cultures. Her work shows how widely correspondence-based practice spread, and why folk traditions should not be assumed to descend from learned magic.

  • Practical application → Folk and traditional magic: Illes documents folk and practical magic across many cultures.
Gemma Gary author · Stage IV

Gary records a regional folk tradition with its own logic of place, season and material. It is studied as a living folk practice, not as a descendant of Renaissance occult philosophy.

  • Practical application → Folk and traditional magic: Gary records the folk magic of Cornwall.
Christopher Warnock author · Stage IV

Warnock teaches how traditional astrology was applied to the making of talismans: the selection of image, material and moment. He shows the reasoning behind practices that Ficino and Agrippa describe in theory.

  • Practical application → Talismanic magic: Warnock teaches the traditional practice of astrological talismans.
  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Picatrix: Warnock co-translated the Picatrix into English with John Michael Greer.

Kabbalah & Qabalah

Sefer Yetzirah text · Stage V

The Book of Formation: creation through ten sefirot and twenty-two letters. Date uncertain, usually placed between the third and sixth centuries.

  • Documented historical influence → Jewish Kabbalah: The Sefer Yetzirah is among the earliest texts of the Jewish esoteric tradition.
Jewish Kabbalah tradition · Stage V

The mystical tradition of Judaism, with its own texts, practices and authorities, including the Sefer Yetzirah and the Zohar.

  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Christian Cabala: Pico della Mirandola and Reuchlin adapted Kabbalistic texts to Christian doctrine.
  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Kabbala Denudata: Knorr von Rosenroth translated Zoharic texts into Latin.
Christian Cabala tradition · Stage V

The Renaissance reading of Kabbalistic texts as confirmations of Christian doctrine, begun by Pico della Mirandola and Johannes Reuchlin.

  • Documented historical influence → Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa: Agrippa lectured on Reuchlin's De verbo mirifico at Dole in 1509 and drew Christian Cabala into his third book. (Agrippa, De occulta philosophia III)
  • Documented historical influence → Hermetic Qabalah: The Hermetic Qabalah inherits the Christian Cabala of the Renaissance, through Agrippa and later writers.
The Tree of Life concept · Stage V

Ten Sephiroth joined by twenty-two paths: the diagram through which unity descends into multiplicity and the soul returns.

  • Educational prerequisite → Rosicrucianism: Case and the Golden Dawn read the Rosicrucian grades on the Tree (Stage V to VI).
Kabbala Denudata text · Stage V

Christian Knorr von Rosenroth's Latin anthology of Zoharic texts (1677–1684), the source of Mathers's Kabbalah Unveiled.

  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Samuel Liddell MacGregor Mathers: Mathers's Kabbalah Unveiled (1887) translates part of the Kabbala Denudata. (Mathers, The Kabbalah Unveiled)
Éliphas Lévi author · Stage V

Lévi is the hinge between Renaissance occult philosophy and the modern occult revival. He presented magic as a coherent philosophy, systematized the doctrine of the Astral Light, and connected the twenty-two Tarot trumps with the twenty-two letters of the Hebrew alphabet. That connection, revised by the Golden Dawn, is the foundation of Hermetic Tarot.

  • Documented historical influence → Hermetic Qabalah: Lévi made the Kabbalah the backbone of a modern philosophy of magic. (Lévi, Dogme et rituel (1854–56))
  • Documented historical influence → Helena P. Blavatsky: Blavatsky cites Lévi in Isis Unveiled. (Blavatsky, Isis Unveiled (1877))
  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Albert Pike: Substantial portions of Morals and Dogma draw on Lévi's writings. (Pike, Morals and Dogma)
  • Documented historical influence → The Golden Dawn: The Golden Dawn developed its attributions of the Tarot to the Hebrew letters from Lévi, with revisions.
  • Documented historical influence → Hermetic Tarot: Lévi joined the twenty-two trumps to the twenty-two Hebrew letters. (Lévi, Dogme et rituel (1854–56))
Hermetic Qabalah tradition · Stage V

The Qabalah of the Western occult revival, developed by Lévi and the Golden Dawn as an organizing system for magic and the Tarot.

  • Philosophical relationship → The Tree of Life: The Tree of Life is the central diagram of the Hermetic Qabalah.
  • Documented historical influence → The Golden Dawn: The Tree of Life organizes the Golden Dawn's grades and correspondences.
  • Practical application → Lesser Ritual of the Pentagram: The Qabalistic Cross and divine names of the ritual are Qabalistic.
  • Educational prerequisite → Hermetic Tarot: The paths of the Tree carry the trumps; the Qabalah must be learned first.
Samuel Liddell MacGregor Mathers author · Stage V

Mathers wrote much of the Golden Dawn's ritual and teaching material, joining Qabalah, Enochian magic, astrology, Tarot and Rosicrucian symbolism into one ceremonial system. <cite>The Kabbalah Unveiled</cite> translates part of Knorr von Rosenroth's <cite>Kabbala Denudata</cite>.

  • Documented historical influence → Hermetic Qabalah: Mathers made the Qabalah the organizing framework of the Golden Dawn system.
  • Documented historical influence → The Golden Dawn: Mathers co-founded the Golden Dawn in 1888 and wrote much of its ritual.
William Wynn Westcott author · Stage V

Westcott links the Masonic and Rosicrucian societies of the nineteenth century to the founding of the Golden Dawn, and his translations made basic Kabbalistic texts available to English readers.

  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Sefer Yetzirah: Westcott translated the Sefer Yetzirah into English (1887).
  • Documented historical influence → Hermetic Qabalah: Westcott's writings and translations shaped the Golden Dawn's Qabalah.
  • Documented historical influence → The Golden Dawn: Westcott co-founded the Golden Dawn in 1888.
Dion Fortune author · Stage V

Fortune presented the Hermetic Qabalah as a practical psychology of the soul, written for the English-speaking student. <cite>The Mystical Qabalah</cite> remains one of the clearest introductions to the Tree of Life as it is used in the Western tradition.

  • Practical application → Hermetic Qabalah: The Mystical Qabalah (1935) presents the Hermetic Qabalah as a practical psychology of the soul.

Alchemy & Rosicrucianism

The Holy Grail concept · Stage IX

The vessel of the medieval romances, sought and attained only by the prepared seeker. See the Objects of Power dossier.

Alchemy tradition · Stage VI

The art of transmutation: a laboratory practice with a long tradition of spiritual interpretation and, later, psychological reading.

  • Documented historical influence → The Philosopher's Stone: The Stone is the goal of the alchemical work.
  • Documented historical influence → Rosicrucianism: The Chymical Wedding is an alchemical allegory; alchemical language pervades the Rosicrucian literature.
The Philosopher's Stone concept · Stage VI

The goal of the alchemical work, read spiritually as the regenerated human being.

  • Philosophical relationship → The Great Work: The Stone is the completion of the Great Work.
The Great Work concept · Stage IX

The alchemical opus, and by extension the whole work of interior transformation.

  • Educational prerequisite → The Lost Word: The final stage returns to the Masonic symbols with the doctrine of regeneration (Stage VI to IX).
Rosicrucianism tradition · Stage VI

The current launched by the manifestos of 1614–1616: a hidden fraternity, the vault of Christian Rosenkreutz and the regeneration of humanity.

  • Comparative symbolic relationship → Freemasonry: Rosicrucian themes appear in several Masonic degrees; the historical relationship between the manifestos and early speculative Freemasonry is uncertain.
  • Textual transmission or adaptation → The Golden Dawn: The Golden Dawn's Adeptus Minor grade enacts the opening of the vault of Christian Rosenkreutz.
The Rosicrucian manifestos text · Stage VI

The Fama (1614), Confessio (1615) and Chymical Wedding (1616).

  • Documented historical influence → Rosicrucianism: The manifestos of 1614–1616 launched the Rosicrucian current.
Michael Maier figure · Stage VI

German physician and alchemist (1568–1622), author of the emblem book Atalanta Fugiens (1617) and an early defender of the Rosicrucians.

  • Documented historical influence → Rosicrucianism: Maier published defenses of the Rosicrucians from 1617.
  • Documented historical influence → Alchemy: Atalanta Fugiens (1617) is one of the great alchemical emblem books.
Arthur Edward Waite author · Stage VI

Waite is both a historian of the Rosicrucian current and the designer of the most influential occult Tarot. His scholarship is cautious about extravagant claims of lineage, which makes him an essential corrective throughout the curriculum.

  • Philosophical relationship → Rosicrucianism: Waite's histories separate the documentary record of the Rosicrucians from legend. (Waite, The Brotherhood of the Rosy Cross (1924))
  • Documented historical influence → Hermetic Tarot: Waite designed the Rider–Waite–Smith deck with Pamela Colman Smith (1909). (Waite, The Pictorial Key to the Tarot (1910))
Paul Foster Case author · Stage VI

Case developed the Qabalistic Tarot into a disciplined method of contemplation, and his Rosicrucian writings connect the Tarot with the grades of the Rosicrucian path.

  • Philosophical relationship → Rosicrucianism: Case interprets the Rosicrucian grades as stages of interior transformation.
  • Documented historical influence → Hermetic Tarot: Case taught the Qabalistic Tarot as a method of contemplation. (Case, The Tarot (1947))
Timothy W. Hogan author · Stage VI

Hogan reads the Masonic degrees through the stages of the alchemical work. In this curriculum his readings are treated as comparative interpretations to be tested against the historical record of both traditions.

  • Comparative symbolic relationship → Freemasonry: Hogan reads the Masonic degrees through the stages of the alchemical work.
  • Comparative symbolic relationship → Alchemy: Hogan's comparison of alchemy and Masonic ritual is treated as a comparative interpretation.

Freemasonry

Freemasonry tradition · Stage I

An initiatory fraternity teaching morality through allegory and symbol, organized in its modern form from the early eighteenth century.

  • Documented historical influence → The Rough Ashlar: The rough and perfect ashlars are emblems of the Craft degrees.
  • Documented historical influence → The Lost Word: The lost word is central to the legend of the third degree.
  • Documented historical influence → The Golden Dawn: The Golden Dawn's founders were Freemasons and members of the Societas Rosicruciana in Anglia, which admitted only Master Masons.
  • Educational prerequisite → Platonism: The Craft raises questions about order, light and the temple that Platonic philosophy addresses (Stage I to II).
The Rough Ashlar concept · Stage I

The unworked stone of the Craft, emblem of the imperfect human nature to be shaped.

  • Comparative symbolic relationship → The Philosopher's Stone: Both images describe the rectification of an imperfect nature. A Greco Arcana comparative interpretation.
The Lost Word concept · Stage IX

The word lost with the death of the master builder in the legend of the third degree, whose recovery the Mason seeks.

  • Comparative symbolic relationship → The Philosopher's Stone: Both name a lost or hidden perfection whose recovery restores the seeker. A comparative interpretation.
  • Comparative symbolic relationship → The Holy Grail: The Grail, the Stone and the Lost Word are studied as parallel symbols. A comparative interpretation.
Albert G. Mackey author · Stage I

Mackey explains why Freemasonry teaches through symbol, allegory and the legend of the lost word. His treatment of symbolism is the foundation on which Stage I is built.

  • Documented historical influence → Freemasonry: Mackey was among the most influential nineteenth-century Masonic scholars. (Mackey, The Symbolism of Freemasonry (1869))
Albert Pike author · Stage I

Pike's lectures on the degrees range across Hermeticism, Qabalah, Gnosticism and comparative religion, and substantial portions of <cite>Morals and Dogma</cite> draw on Éliphas Lévi. He demonstrates how Freemasonry was read in the nineteenth century as a doorway into the wider Western mysteries.

  • Documented historical influence → Freemasonry: Pike led the Southern Jurisdiction of the Scottish Rite from 1859 to 1891. (Pike, Morals and Dogma (1871))
Walter Leslie Wilmshurst author · Stage I

Wilmshurst reads the Craft degrees as a map of spiritual regeneration, treating the lodge as an image of the human being. He is the clearest bridge between the moral instruction of the lodge and the interior work of the mysteries.

  • Philosophical relationship → Freemasonry: Wilmshurst interprets the degrees as a map of spiritual regeneration. (Wilmshurst, The Meaning of Masonry (1922))
Manly P. Hall author · Stage I

Hall introduced generations of readers to the symbolic unity of the Western traditions. His early book on Freemasonry treats the Craft as the custodian of a philosophical inheritance, which is exactly the question Stage I asks the student to examine.

  • Philosophical relationship → Freemasonry: Hall reads the Craft as the custodian of a philosophical inheritance. (Hall, The Lost Keys of Freemasonry (1923))

Ceremonial magic

Enochian magic tradition · Stage VII

The angelic system recorded in the sessions of John Dee and Edward Kelley and later adapted by the Golden Dawn.

  • Textual transmission or adaptation → The Golden Dawn: The Golden Dawn adapted the Enochian material from Dee's manuscripts.
  • Practical application → Ceremonial magic: The angelic tradition is one of the currents of modern ceremonial magic.
John Dee author · Stage VII

Dee's angelic records, published in part by Meric Casaubon in 1659, are the source of the Enochian system later adapted by the Golden Dawn. He embodies the Renaissance conviction that mathematics, philosophy and theurgy belong to one inquiry.

  • Documented historical influence → Enochian magic: The Enochian system derives from the records of Dee and Kelley's sessions. (Casaubon, A True and Faithful Relation (1659))
The Golden Dawn tradition · Stage VII

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, founded in London in 1888, which synthesized the Qabalah, astrology, Enochian magic, Tarot and Rosicrucian symbolism.

  • Documented historical influence → Aleister Crowley: Crowley was initiated into the Golden Dawn in 1898.
  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Israel Regardie: Regardie published the Golden Dawn's teachings in 1937–1940. (Regardie, The Golden Dawn)
  • Philosophical relationship → John Michael Greer: Greer has written extensively on the Golden Dawn tradition and adapted it in his own systems.
  • Documented historical influence → Dion Fortune: Fortune was trained in a Golden Dawn successor order before founding the Fraternity of the Inner Light.
  • Documented historical influence → Arthur Edward Waite: Waite joined the Golden Dawn in 1891.
  • Documented historical influence → Paul Foster Case: Case joined a Golden Dawn successor temple before founding the Builders of the Adytum.
  • Documented historical influence → Lesser Ritual of the Pentagram: The Lesser Ritual of the Pentagram is taught in the Golden Dawn's first grade material.
  • Documented historical influence → Ceremonial magic: The Golden Dawn produced the most influential modern synthesis of ceremonial magic.
  • Documented historical influence → Hermetic Tarot: The Golden Dawn revised Lévi's attributions and assigned the cards to the Tree, the planets and the decans.
Lesser Ritual of the Pentagram concept · Stage VII

The Golden Dawn's basic ritual, in which several philosophical systems converge in a single operation (Figure 18).

  • Practical application → Ceremonial magic: The pentagram ritual is a basic operation of the ceremonial art.
Ceremonial magic tradition · Stage VII

The operative art in which the currents of this curriculum converge: theurgy, Hermeticism, astrology, natural magic, Qabalah, Rosicrucianism and angelic magic.

Thelema tradition · Stage VII

The religious and magical system founded by Aleister Crowley on The Book of the Law (1904).

Aleister Crowley author · Stage VII

Crowley reworked the Golden Dawn's ceremonial synthesis into his own system and wrote one of the most thorough modern treatments of magical theory and practice. He is studied here for that technical contribution, with the critical distance any serious student applies to a controversial figure.

  • Documented historical influence → Thelema: Crowley founded Thelema on The Book of the Law (1904).
  • Documented historical influence → Israel Regardie: Regardie served as Crowley's secretary from 1928.
Israel Regardie author · Stage VII

Regardie made the Golden Dawn's curriculum available to the solitary student. Almost every later Golden Dawn practitioner, including the Ciceros, works from the corpus he published.

  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Chic Cicero: The Ciceros revised and edited later editions of Regardie's The Golden Dawn.
  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Sandra Tabatha Cicero: The Ciceros revised and edited later editions of Regardie's The Golden Dawn.
Franz Bardon author · Stage VII

Bardon offers a graded, self-directed training in concentration, elemental balance and magical practice. It is included as an independent operative system rather than as part of the Golden Dawn lineage.

  • Practical application → Ceremonial magic: Bardon's graded training is an independent operative system, not part of the Golden Dawn lineage.
Chic Cicero author · Stage VII

The Ciceros adapted the Golden Dawn curriculum for the solitary student, which places their work directly beside the purpose of The Solitary Study.

Sandra Tabatha Cicero author · Stage VII

The Ciceros adapted the Golden Dawn curriculum for the solitary student, which places their work directly beside the purpose of The Solitary Study.

Jason Miller author · Stage VII

Miller writes for the practitioner who wants results in ordinary life. His work is included as an additional operative approach, not as part of a Golden Dawn lineage.

  • Practical application → Ceremonial magic: Miller writes as a contemporary practitioner of practical magic.
John Michael Greer author · Stage VII

Greer combines careful occult history with practical training. <cite>The Way of the Golden Section</cite> joins ceremonial practice with sacred geometry, showing how the geometrical studies of Stage II return in operative form.

  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Picatrix: Greer co-translated the Picatrix into English with Christopher Warnock.
  • Practical application → Sacred geometry: The Way of the Golden Section joins ceremonial training with sacred geometry.

Comparative esotericism

Theosophy tradition · Stage V

The movement founded by Blavatsky, Olcott and Judge in 1875, with a comparative esoteric cosmology.

  • Documented historical influence → Rudolf Steiner: Steiner led the German section of the Theosophical Society from 1902.
Helena P. Blavatsky author · Stage V

Blavatsky popularized a comparative esoteric cosmology that drew Eastern and Western sources into one system and shaped much of the twentieth-century occult revival. In this curriculum she is read comparatively, as an influential esoteric cosmology rather than an authority on traditional Jewish Kabbalah.

  • Comparative symbolic relationship → The Tree of Life: Read comparatively: Blavatsky's cosmology of emanation is set beside the Qabalistic model.
  • Documented historical influence → Theosophy: Blavatsky co-founded the Theosophical Society in 1875.
Rudolf Steiner author · Stage V

Steiner's cosmology is studied comparatively, as a modern Western esoteric system with its own account of the human constitution and the evolution of consciousness, distinct from traditional Kabbalah.

  • Comparative symbolic relationship → The Tree of Life: Read comparatively: Steiner's account of the human constitution is set beside the Qabalistic parts of the soul.
  • Documented historical influence → Anthroposophy: Steiner founded the Anthroposophical Society in 1912–1913.
Anthroposophy tradition · Stage V

Tarot

Historical Tarot tradition · Stage VIII

The Tarot as created in fifteenth-century Italy, a card game whose trumps drew on Renaissance imagery.

  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Hermetic Tarot: The occult reading of the Tarot was applied to a card game created in fifteenth-century Italy.
Hermetic Tarot tradition · Stage VIII

The occult reading of the Tarot developed by Lévi, the Golden Dawn, Waite and Case, joining the cards to the Hebrew letters, astrology and the Tree of Life.

Rachel Pollack author · Stage VIII

Pollack's reading of the Rider–Waite–Smith images is among the most influential modern interpretations of the deck, joining the traditional correspondences with psychological and mythic insight.

  • Practical application → Hermetic Tarot: Pollack's interpretation develops the Rider–Waite–Smith images.
Mary K. Greer author · Stage VIII

Greer teaches the Tarot as a practice of self-reflection and personal interpretation, which is the skill Stage VIII asks the student to develop on top of the traditional correspondences.

  • Practical application → Hermetic Tarot: Greer teaches the practice of reading for oneself.
Paul Huson author · Stage IV

Huson's history of the Tarot traces the cards' imagery to medieval and Renaissance sources before the occult reinterpretations of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, which keeps Stage VIII historically honest.

  • Practical application → Folk and traditional magic: Mastering Witchcraft (1970) reshapes grimoire and folk material for the witchcraft revival.
  • Philosophical relationship → Historical Tarot: Huson traces the cards' imagery to medieval and Renaissance sources.
