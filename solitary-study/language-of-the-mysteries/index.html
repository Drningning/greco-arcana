Stage VIII Lingua Mysteriorum

The Language of the Mysteries

How can the greater tradition be expressed and interpreted through images?

i. The Threshold

In Paris in 1781, the scholar Antoine Court de Gébelin published the eighth volume of his vast study of the primitive world. In it he announced that the Tarot, a card game then played in parts of Europe, was in fact an ancient Egyptian book, the Book of Thoth, preserved in disguise by people who did not know what they held.

He was wrong. The Tarot was created in northern Italy in the fifteenth century as a card game, and its trumps drew on the familiar imagery of Renaissance Christian culture. But Court de Gébelin's error proved extraordinarily fruitful. Within a century Éliphas Lévi had joined the trumps to the Hebrew letters, and within another generation the Golden Dawn had built the cards into the most complete symbolic language of the Western mysteries.

The student of Hermetic Tarot needs to hold both truths at once: the cards have a documented history that predates their occult reading, and that occult reading is itself a real and serious tradition.

ii. The Purpose

This stage teaches Hermetic Tarot as an interpretive language, not as a list of meanings to memorize. You will study the history of the cards, their occult reinterpretation, the structure of the Major and Minor Arcana and the correspondences of Hebrew letters, astrology, elements and the Tree of Life (Figures 19 and 20).

The particular system studied here is Hermetic Tarot, the tradition of Lévi, the Golden Dawn, Waite and Case. It is not every form of Tarot reading, and the student should understand where it differs from historical and other modern approaches.

The worked example is the Three of Swords (Figure 21). Its Rider–Waite–Smith image of a heart pierced by three swords under rain is familiar. Behind it stand Binah, the third Sephirah; the suit of Swords and the element of Air; the decan of Saturn in Libra assigned to it by the Golden Dawn; and the Golden Dawn title Lord of Sorrow. A student who knows the preceding stages can read all of these layers together and arrive at an interpretation, rather than recalling a keyword.

iv. The Intellectual Architecture

Begin with history. Paul Huson's Mystical Origins of the Tarot traces the cards' imagery to medieval and Renaissance sources. Then return to Lévi, whose attribution of the trumps to the Hebrew letters began the Hermetic reading, and which the Golden Dawn later revised.

Waite's Pictorial Key is the guide to the deck he designed with Pamela Colman Smith, published by Rider in 1909. It is deliberately reticent about the Golden Dawn correspondences behind its images. Paul Foster Case states those correspondences openly and teaches the trumps as instruments of contemplation. Rachel Pollack and Mary K. Greer develop the craft of interpretation, joining traditional symbolism with mythic and personal insight.

The map for this stage shows why Hermetic Tarot comes so late in the curriculum. Select "Hermetic Tarot" and its lines run back to astrology, the Hermetic Qabalah, elemental symbolism, Lévi, the Golden Dawn, Waite and Case. The cards are a summary of the tradition, and they can only be read fully once the tradition is known.

Astrology, natural & folk magic

Traditional astrology tradition · Stage IV

The traditional science of the seven planets, the zodiac, the houses and the choice of times.

  • Practical application → The Golden Dawn: Astrological correspondences are integral to Golden Dawn ritual and teaching.
  • Practical application → Hermetic Tarot: The minor arcana and trumps carry astrological attributions in Hermetic Tarot.

Kabbalah & Qabalah

Éliphas Lévi author · Stage V

Lévi is the hinge between Renaissance occult philosophy and the modern occult revival. He presented magic as a coherent philosophy, systematized the doctrine of the Astral Light, and connected the twenty-two Tarot trumps with the twenty-two letters of the Hebrew alphabet. That connection, revised by the Golden Dawn, is the foundation of Hermetic Tarot.

  • Documented historical influence → Hermetic Qabalah: Lévi made the Kabbalah the backbone of a modern philosophy of magic. (Lévi, Dogme et rituel (1854–56))
  • Documented historical influence → The Golden Dawn: The Golden Dawn developed its attributions of the Tarot to the Hebrew letters from Lévi, with revisions.
  • Documented historical influence → Hermetic Tarot: Lévi joined the twenty-two trumps to the twenty-two Hebrew letters. (Lévi, Dogme et rituel (1854–56))
Hermetic Qabalah tradition · Stage V

The Qabalah of the Western occult revival, developed by Lévi and the Golden Dawn as an organizing system for magic and the Tarot.

  • Documented historical influence → The Golden Dawn: The Tree of Life organizes the Golden Dawn's grades and correspondences.
  • Educational prerequisite → Hermetic Tarot: The paths of the Tree carry the trumps; the Qabalah must be learned first.

Alchemy & Rosicrucianism

Arthur Edward Waite author · Stage VI

Waite is both a historian of the Rosicrucian current and the designer of the most influential occult Tarot. His scholarship is cautious about extravagant claims of lineage, which makes him an essential corrective throughout the curriculum.

  • Documented historical influence → Hermetic Tarot: Waite designed the Rider–Waite–Smith deck with Pamela Colman Smith (1909). (Waite, The Pictorial Key to the Tarot (1910))
Paul Foster Case author · Stage VI

Case developed the Qabalistic Tarot into a disciplined method of contemplation, and his Rosicrucian writings connect the Tarot with the grades of the Rosicrucian path.

  • Documented historical influence → Hermetic Tarot: Case taught the Qabalistic Tarot as a method of contemplation. (Case, The Tarot (1947))

Ceremonial magic

The Golden Dawn tradition · Stage VII

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, founded in London in 1888, which synthesized the Qabalah, astrology, Enochian magic, Tarot and Rosicrucian symbolism.

  • Documented historical influence → Arthur Edward Waite: Waite joined the Golden Dawn in 1891.
  • Documented historical influence → Paul Foster Case: Case joined a Golden Dawn successor temple before founding the Builders of the Adytum.
  • Documented historical influence → Hermetic Tarot: The Golden Dawn revised Lévi's attributions and assigned the cards to the Tree, the planets and the decans.

Tarot

Historical Tarot tradition · Stage VIII

The Tarot as created in fifteenth-century Italy, a card game whose trumps drew on Renaissance imagery.

  • Textual transmission or adaptation → Hermetic Tarot: The occult reading of the Tarot was applied to a card game created in fifteenth-century Italy.
Hermetic Tarot tradition · Stage VIII

The occult reading of the Tarot developed by Lévi, the Golden Dawn, Waite and Case, joining the cards to the Hebrew letters, astrology and the Tree of Life.

Rachel Pollack author · Stage VIII

Pollack's reading of the Rider–Waite–Smith images is among the most influential modern interpretations of the deck, joining the traditional correspondences with psychological and mythic insight.

  • Practical application → Hermetic Tarot: Pollack's interpretation develops the Rider–Waite–Smith images.
Mary K. Greer author · Stage VIII

Greer teaches the Tarot as a practice of self-reflection and personal interpretation, which is the skill Stage VIII asks the student to develop on top of the traditional correspondences.

  • Practical application → Hermetic Tarot: Greer teaches the practice of reading for oneself.
Paul Huson author · Stage IV

Huson's history of the Tarot traces the cards' imagery to medieval and Renaissance sources before the occult reinterpretations of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, which keeps Stage VIII historically honest.

  • Philosophical relationship → Historical Tarot: Huson traces the cards' imagery to medieval and Renaissance sources.

vi. The Masonic Connection

Documented Waite, who designed the Rider–Waite–Smith deck, was a Freemason who wrote extensively on Masonic history, and Masonic and Rosicrucian imagery appears in the Golden Dawn's Tarot. The two pillars of the High Priestess, marked B and J, echo the pillars of Solomon's porch.

Greco Arcana interpretation The Tarot and the Masonic tracing board work in the same way: a single image condenses a body of teaching that the student unfolds by contemplation. A Freemason who has learned to read a tracing board has already learned the method of reading a trump. This is our comparative observation.

vii. The Work

These exercises develop understanding; they promise no supernatural results. Record each in your study journal.

  1. Comparative reading

    History and interpretation

    Choose three trumps. For each, write what the image meant in its fifteenth-century setting, according to Huson, and what the Golden Dawn and Case later read into it.

  2. Symbolic analysis

    The Three of Swords

    Using Figure 21, write an interpretation of the Three of Swords that draws on all five layers: Binah, Air, Saturn in Libra, the Golden Dawn title and the Rider–Waite–Smith image. Then write a second interpretation for a specific question, showing how the layers guide you.

  3. Journaling

    Daily card

    Draw one card each morning for the length of the stage. Record its correspondences and your interpretation, and in the evening note what, if anything, the day revealed. Review the journal at the end of the stage.

  4. Discursive meditation

    Contemplating a trump

    Following Case's method, contemplate one trump for twenty minutes a day for a week: its image, its Hebrew letter, its path on the Tree. Record what arises.

viii. Standard of Completion

Advance because you understand the material, not because you have finished reading it.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the historical development of the Tarot and its later occult reinterpretation.
  • Explain the structure of the Major and Minor Arcana and their Qabalistic, astrological and elemental correspondences.
  • Distinguish Hermetic Tarot from other forms of Tarot reading.
  • Construct a coherent Hermetic Tarot interpretation using multiple layers of symbolism.

Questions for self-assessment

  1. Why was Court de Gébelin wrong, and why did his error matter?
  2. Can you explain the Three of Swords through all five of its layers?
  3. What did the Golden Dawn change in Lévi's attribution of the trumps?
  4. How does knowing the preceding stages change the way you read a card?
