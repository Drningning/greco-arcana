The Rocket Scientist Who Invoked a Goddess

Jack Parsons, Aleister Crowley, L. Ron Hubbard and the Babalon Working of 1946.

One of the men who helped humanity reach the heavens was simultaneously attempting to bring a goddess down from them.

There are certain historical associations that sound so improbable that one instinctively assumes they belong to fiction.

The origins of the American space program. The founder of Scientology. The notorious English magician Aleister Crowley. A series of ceremonial magical operations intended to manifest a divine feminine force upon Earth.

All of these converge in the life of one extraordinary American.

His name was John Whiteside Parsons, better known as Jack.

By profession, he was a pioneer of rocket propulsion.

By conviction, he was a ceremonial magician.

In 1946, he undertook an occult operation whose consequences continue to be debated within the Western Mystery Tradition.

It became known as the Babalon Working.

The man who pursued the heavens

Born in 1914, Parsons developed an early fascination with the possibility of space travel.

During the 1930s, rocketry occupied an uncertain position between legitimate scientific investigation and science fiction. The prospect of sending machinery beyond Earth's atmosphere was regarded by many as an impractical fantasy.

Parsons thought otherwise.

Working alongside pioneering researchers associated with the California Institute of Technology, he helped develop technologies that would eventually contribute to the foundations of modern American rocketry.

He became a co-founder of Aerojet, and the experimental research group with which he worked developed into what we now know as the Jet Propulsion Laboratory.

His scientific accomplishments were substantial.

Yet his interest in reaching beyond the limitations of the material world was not confined to engineering.

Parsons had become equally fascinated by the possibility that human consciousness could transcend its ordinary limitations through ceremonial magic.

For him, these were not necessarily contradictory pursuits.

Both concerned the exploration of possibilities that conventional society had dismissed.

The magician of Pasadena

During the early 1940s, Parsons became involved with the Ordo Templi Orientis, an initiatory organization closely associated with Aleister Crowley and his religious and philosophical system known as Thelema.

Crowley's teachings placed considerable emphasis upon the discovery and accomplishment of one's True Will.

This concept is frequently misunderstood as permission to indulge every personal desire.

Within Thelemic philosophy, however, True Will is more properly understood as the fundamental purpose or course of an individual's existence, distinguished from the temporary appetites and conflicts of the personality.

Parsons embraced the tradition with considerable enthusiasm.

He eventually assumed leadership responsibilities within the Agape Lodge, operating from his residence in Pasadena.

The house became a gathering place for artists, intellectuals, occultists and unconventional personalities.

Among those who entered Parsons' circle was a science-fiction writer and former naval officer whose name would eventually become internationally recognizable.

L. Ron Hubbard.

The arrival of Hubbard

Hubbard entered Parsons' household in 1945.

Parsons was impressed by his apparent aptitude for occult work, and the two developed a close association.

Their relationship extended beyond intellectual discussion. Hubbard began participating in Parsons' magical experiments, serving as a seer and recorder of experiences during ceremonial operations.

In early 1946, Parsons initiated a series of workings intended to establish contact with a particular manifestation of the divine feminine.

The operation drew upon Thelemic teachings, ceremonial magic, Enochian material and ideas concerning the relationship between spiritual forces and their manifestation within the physical world.

Its ambitions would soon extend beyond the attraction of a magical partner.

Parsons sought to establish the conditions through which a greater spiritual force might manifest within human existence.

That force was Babalon.

Who was Babalon?

To understand what Parsons believed he was accomplishing, it is necessary to distinguish Babalon from the figure that inspired her name.

In the Book of Revelation, Babylon appears as a woman associated with a scarlet beast, a golden cup and the corruption of worldly power.

Crowley's esoteric system radically reinterpreted this imagery.

Within Thelema, Babalon became a sacred expression of the divine feminine, associated with unrestricted experience, the dissolution of egoic boundaries and the reception of individual existence into a greater spiritual whole.

These themes are developed particularly in Crowley's The Vision and the Voice, a record of his exploration of the Enochian Aethyrs.

Rather than simply representing an embodiment of wickedness, Babalon became associated with a profound initiatory mystery.

In the symbolism of Thelema, the individual approaching the Abyss must relinquish the rigid identification with a separate, self-governing personality.

Babalon receives that which the individual can no longer retain as exclusively his own.

Her cup, therefore, has an important symbolic function.

It represents reception, surrender and the dissolution of separateness.

For Parsons, however, Babalon was considerably more than a philosophical abstraction.

He believed her influence could be brought into manifestation.

The woman who appeared

Following the initial phase of his magical operations, Parsons encountered Marjorie Cameron.

Cameron was a former member of the United States Naval Reserve and would eventually become a distinctive artist and figure within the American occult underground.

Parsons interpreted her arrival as the fulfillment of his earlier magical work.

Her appearance and temperament corresponded sufficiently closely with his expectations that he believed the operation had succeeded.

There is no independent evidence that Cameron's arrival was caused by ceremonial magic.

Indeed, Cameron initially had little understanding of the significance Parsons attributed to her appearance.

Nevertheless, in Parsons' interpretation, the encounter marked an extraordinary development.

What had previously existed as an intention within the magician's consciousness now appeared to possess a corresponding expression in the material world.

Parsons and Cameron subsequently participated in further magical work, and their relationship developed into marriage later that year.

It is important, however, that Cameron not be reduced to a character in Parsons' magical narrative. She was an independent individual whose artistic and spiritual life continued long after his death.

The Babalon Working

During the subsequent phases of the operation, Parsons pursued the manifestation of Babalon's influence through a series of ceremonial and visionary experiences.

These workings included practices drawn from the sexual magic associated with particular currents of Thelema.

Parsons also produced a short text known as Liber 49, or The Book of Babalon.

He understood this text to be a communication from Babalon herself.

Its significance, in his estimation, extended beyond his personal spiritual development. Parsons believed he was participating in the introduction of a transformative spiritual influence into the modern world.

To him, the workings concerned the emergence of a new relationship between human freedom, sexuality, spiritual authority and the divine feminine.

Yet not everyone within his own tradition shared his enthusiasm.

Crowley's reservations

Aleister Crowley, who was living in England, received correspondence concerning Parsons' activities.

His reaction was far from an enthusiastic endorsement.

Crowley expressed serious reservations about Parsons' judgment and the involvement of Hubbard.

This is particularly revealing when one considers Crowley's own reputation.

The man whose writings had provided much of the intellectual and magical foundation for Parsons' undertaking was himself skeptical of the way those teachings were being applied.

The episode exposes a disagreement that remains relevant within ceremonial magic today.

Does proficiency in magical technique necessarily indicate spiritual maturity?

Can a magician distinguish the manifestation of a spiritual intelligence from the projection of his own desires?

And what happens when the magician becomes emotionally invested in a particular result?

Parsons was convinced that something extraordinary had occurred.

Crowley was considerably less convinced that Parsons understood what he was doing.

The aftermath

The relationships surrounding the Babalon Working eventually fractured.

A business venture involving Parsons and Hubbard ended in a serious financial and legal dispute.

Hubbard departed and would later establish Dianetics and Scientology.

Parsons married Cameron in October 1946 and continued his involvement in both scientific and occult pursuits.

In June 1952, at the age of thirty-seven, Parsons died following an explosion in his home laboratory.

His death has attracted considerable speculation, including attempts to connect it with his magical activities.

No reliable evidence establishes such a supernatural connection.

His scientific work, however, survived him.

Decades later, technologies descended from the field he helped pioneer carried human instruments into the very heavens that had fascinated him since childhood.

The practitioner's perspective

What makes the Babalon Working particularly interesting is not simply that an accomplished scientist practiced ceremonial magic.

It is that Parsons appears to have approached both disciplines with a similar conviction: that invisible processes could be understood, engaged and brought into manifestation.

The alchemist works with substances in the laboratory, but the Great Work concerns more than those substances.

The ceremonial magician works with symbols, names, gestures and images, but the significance of those instruments extends beyond their physical appearance.

Within Hermetic philosophy, correspondence is not merely the observation that one thing resembles another.

It concerns the possibility that phenomena which appear separate within the manifest world may participate in a deeper underlying relationship.

A symbol may therefore provide access to something greater than the image itself.

Parsons appears to have taken this possibility extraordinarily seriously.

But his story also illustrates one of the greatest difficulties of occult practice.

How does one establish that a correspondence has been discovered rather than imposed?

Imagine a magician who undertakes an operation to establish contact with a particular spiritual force. Shortly afterward, a stranger enters his life possessing characteristics associated with that force.

There are several possible explanations.

The event might represent a meaningful correspondence. It might be coincidence. It might be the magician's own expectations causing him to interpret an otherwise ordinary encounter as extraordinary.

The magician's enthusiasm cannot, by itself, resolve the question.

Here, the importance of equilibrium becomes apparent.

Consider the High Priestess of the Tarot.

She sits before the veil, between the pillars. She is intimately associated with hidden knowledge, yet she maintains her position. She neither abandons discernment in pursuit of revelation nor denies the existence of that which remains concealed.

Her position is one of balance.

The magician who encounters an apparent manifestation must exercise comparable discipline.

An extraordinary experience is not necessarily evidence of an extraordinary explanation.

The Babalon Working remains fascinating because it presents us with a man who attempted to establish a relationship between an invisible spiritual principle and a tangible manifestation within the world.

Whether he accomplished what he believed he had accomplished remains unresolved.

Perhaps the greatest lesson of his work is not that humanity can summon a goddess.

It is that before attempting to distinguish the gods from the contents of one's own consciousness, one might benefit considerably from knowing oneself.
