The Astrologer in the White House

Nancy Reagan, Joan Quigley and the celestial calculations behind an American presidency.

Period

Location

Electional and Mundane Astrology

For seven years, one of the most powerful men in the world had an astrologer advising on his schedule. The American public had absolutely no idea.

During the 1980s, the United States possessed an extraordinary intelligence apparatus. Satellites monitored foreign governments. Military advisers evaluated international threats. Economists studied markets, diplomats negotiated treaties and the Secret Service protected the president.

Yet alongside this enormous machinery of modern government, there was another source of counsel.

A woman living in San Francisco was studying the movements of the planets.

Her name was Joan Quigley.

Her client was Nancy Reagan.

And her work would bring one of the oldest occult sciences into the administrative affairs of the most powerful nation on Earth.

The day everything changed

On March 30, 1981, an assassination attempt nearly ended Ronald Reagan's presidency just weeks after his inauguration.

For Nancy Reagan, the event was devastating. Her husband's position required him to appear before crowds, travel extensively and expose himself to circumstances that even the most sophisticated security apparatus could not entirely control.

She began seeking an additional means of protection.

Through television personality Merv Griffin, Nancy had previously become acquainted with Joan Quigley, an astrologer from a prominent San Francisco family.

Following the assassination attempt, Nancy contacted Quigley. She wanted to know whether the danger could have been anticipated astrologically.

Quigley believed that it could have been.

What followed was an extraordinary private arrangement that would continue through much of the Reagan presidency.

The woman behind the celestial calculations

Joan Quigley was not an anonymous fortune teller operating from a storefront.

Born in 1927, she was educated at Vassar College, had authored several books on astrology and appeared regularly on American television.

Her approach involved detailed calculations of planetary positions, natal charts, eclipses, lunations and planetary cycles.

The distinction is important.

Nancy Reagan was not necessarily asking Quigley to predict every event in her husband's future. She was asking her to identify periods during which particular activities might be more or less favorable.

This practice belongs to a branch of traditional astrology considerably older than the familiar newspaper horoscope.

It is called electional astrology.

Choosing the moment

Electional astrology concerns the selection of an appropriate time to undertake an action.

Rather than asking what will happen tomorrow, the electional astrologer asks when a particular intention might best be put into motion.

Historically, astrologers were consulted regarding marriages, coronations, journeys, commercial ventures, the construction of buildings and military campaigns.

The underlying premise is that the moment at which an undertaking begins possesses qualities relevant to its subsequent development.

Within the Western esoteric tradition, time is not necessarily regarded as a collection of interchangeable units.

One hour may be considered more suitable for reconciliation, another for commerce, another for contemplation.

The astrologer attempts to recognize these qualities and select a moment corresponding to the undertaking.

It was this ancient approach to time that found its way into the Reagan White House.

A calendar written in the stars

According to accounts published after the arrangement became public, Quigley advised Nancy Reagan concerning favorable and unfavorable periods for presidential activities.

These included travel, public appearances, press conferences and diplomatic engagements.

Quigley would later claim that her recommendations extended to the timing of presidential debates, international summits and even the movements of Air Force One.

The precise extent of her influence remains disputed.

But the existence of the arrangement does not.

Imagine the administrative predicament.

A presidential engagement might be acceptable to the State Department, the Secret Service and the White House staff, yet encounter an additional objection rooted in the relationship between Mars, Saturn and the president's natal chart.

An ancient discipline had acquired an unexpected place within modern American government.

Astrology and the Cold War

Among Quigley's most extraordinary claims were those concerning Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev.

The Cold War was entering a critical period. Diplomatic relations between the Soviet Union and the United States carried consequences for the entire world.

Quigley maintained that her astrological assessment of Reagan and Gorbachev suggested considerable potential for cooperation between the two leaders.

She claimed to have encouraged a less confrontational approach toward the Soviet Union.

If her account is accepted, astrology was no longer being used exclusively to select appropriate moments. It had become a means of assessing the relationship between two individuals whose decisions could profoundly affect international affairs.

However, this is where the historical record requires particular care.

Nancy Reagan disputed the suggestion that political decisions were determined astrologically. Quigley's influence over presidential policy cannot be established simply from her own retrospective claims.

The distinction between advising on the timing of diplomacy and determining its substance is considerable.

Nevertheless, both possibilities entered public discussion when the arrangement was finally exposed.

The secret becomes public

In 1988, former White House Chief of Staff Donald Regan published his memoir, For the Record.

Among its revelations was the extraordinary disclosure that an astrologer had influenced the scheduling of presidential activities.

The story became an international embarrassment for the administration.

Newspapers ridiculed the arrangement. Political commentators questioned its implications. The involvement of astrology appeared incompatible with the public image of a technologically sophisticated modern government.

Nancy Reagan subsequently acknowledged the consultations in her autobiography, My Turn, while insisting that astrology had not determined governmental policy.

Quigley published her own account in 1990, entitled What Does Joan Say?

The three accounts offered different assessments of the astrologer's importance.

What emerged was an unusual historical certainty surrounded by considerable uncertainty.

The astrologer was real. The consultations were real. The disagreements concerned how much influence those consultations actually exercised.

The esoteric tradition

The ancient tradition behind the controversy

The presence of an astrologer near political power would hardly have surprised a Renaissance scholar.

John Dee advised Elizabeth I. Medieval and Renaissance courts employed learned astrologers. The timing of significant political and religious undertakings had been a subject of astrological investigation for centuries.

Traditional astrology also developed a branch known as mundane astrology, concerned with nations, governments, political events and large-scale historical developments.

Within this intellectual framework, the individual, the state and the heavens were understood as potentially participating in a larger order.

The modern scientific worldview does not establish that planetary positions can reliably predict political events or determine their outcomes.

But historically, astrology was not regarded exclusively as a pastime. It was a sophisticated system through which human beings attempted to understand their relationship with time, nature and the cosmos.

What happened during the Reagan presidency was an unexpected reappearance of that older worldview.

The practitioner's perspective

There is an important distinction between using astrology to determine what one should do and using astrology to determine when one should do it.

The former can become an abdication of personal judgment.

The latter is an attempt to understand the circumstances surrounding an action whose purpose has already been established.

Consider a gardener.

He does not ask the seasons what he should plant. He determines what he wishes to cultivate, and then considers when the conditions might be most favorable for its growth.

The traditional electional astrologer approaches human endeavor through a comparable principle.

Within Hermetic philosophy, correspondence suggests that apparently separate phenomena may participate in a deeper underlying relationship. Astrology represents one historical attempt to understand and work with such relationships.

But there is a danger when the instrument of discernment becomes an instrument of dependency.

Nancy Reagan's understandable desire to protect her husband raises a larger philosophical question.

At what point does seeking knowledge of favorable circumstances become an attempt to eliminate the uncertainty inherent in human existence?

There is nothing inherently contradictory about consulting an astrologer while exercising independent judgment.

The difficulty arises when judgment is surrendered altogether.

Perhaps the most important lesson of the White House astrologer is not whether Joan Quigley could read the heavens.

It is whether those consulting her could distinguish the search for wisdom from the search for certainty.

The two are not the same.
