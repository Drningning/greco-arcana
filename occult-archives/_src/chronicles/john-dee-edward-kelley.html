In the spring of 1587, one of England's most accomplished intellectuals received an extraordinary instruction from what he believed to be a heavenly messenger.

The angels demanded that he share his wife with another man.

The intellectual was Dr. John Dee, a mathematician, astrologer, natural philosopher, and adviser to Queen Elizabeth I. The other man was Edward Kelley, an alchemist who had spent years serving as Dee's intermediary between the visible and invisible worlds.

This is not an obscure legend passed down through generations of occultists. Dee documented the affair himself.

What makes the story particularly disturbing, however, is not the proposition itself, but the spiritual reasoning by which two educated men came to entertain it.

The Queen's Magician

To understand what happened in 1587, we must first abandon our modern distinction between the scientist and the magician.

During the Renaissance, mathematics, astronomy, astrology, alchemy, theology and magic often belonged to the same intellectual enterprise: understanding the architecture of creation.

John Dee was one of its most ambitious students.

His work on Euclidean geometry, his interest in navigation, and his extensive studies of mathematics and natural philosophy had established his scholarly reputation. Yet Dee believed that conventional learning could take humanity only so far.

If creation was the work of an intelligent Creator, then surely its underlying principles could be discovered.

And who better to explain the architecture of creation than those beings believed to stand closer to its Architect?

The angels.

Dee's interest in angelic communication was therefore not a departure from his intellectual pursuits. It was an extension of them. His magical work represented an attempt to obtain knowledge that ordinary scholarship had failed to deliver.

The Man Who Could See

{{figure:kelley|right|Edward Kelley (1555–1597)|English alchemist, scryer and collaborator in the development of what became Enochian magic.}}

There was, however, a practical problem.

Dee struggled to perceive the beings with whom he wished to communicate.

In 1582, he encountered Edward Kelley, a considerably younger man who claimed to possess precisely the faculty Dee lacked.

Kelley would serve as the scryer, employing a reflective or crystalline object through which he reported perceiving spiritual beings. Dee would question them and meticulously record the answers.

Their collaboration produced an extraordinary body of material: elaborate tables of letters, unfamiliar words, angelic names, cosmological descriptions and ritual instructions.

Whether one regards these communications as supernatural revelations, psychological phenomena or elaborate inventions, their complexity is remarkable.

But the arrangement contained a fundamental weakness.

Dee could question the angels, study their instructions and interpret their teachings.

He could not independently hear what they were saying.

Between Dee and the heavens stood Edward Kelley.

The Road to Bohemia

In 1583, Dee and Kelley departed England with their households.

Their travels brought them into the intellectual and aristocratic circles of Central Europe, including those surrounding Rudolf II, the Holy Roman Emperor whose fascination with alchemy and the occult helped make Prague a center of Renaissance esotericism.

{{figure:prague|wide}}

Eventually, the two men found themselves under the patronage of the Bohemian nobleman Vilém of Rožmberk.

By this time, their interests were increasingly divergent. Kelley was developing a reputation as an alchemist, while Dee remained committed to his spiritual investigations.

Their relationship had become strained. Kelley had expressed a desire to abandon his role as scryer, and Dee's household was suffering under the burdens of their prolonged undertaking.

The experiment had ceased to be merely an intellectual adventure. Entire families were living with its consequences.

Then came the revelation that would overshadow almost everything else.

The Instruction of April 1587

During a spiritual consultation on April 18, 1587, Kelley reported an unsettling communication.

The two men were instructed to hold their possessions in common.

This included their wives.

The revelation was associated with a spiritual figure appearing in their records under the name Madimi, an entity familiar to their earlier workings.

Dee was troubled by the instruction. Kelley himself expressed resistance to the proposition, although historians have questioned whether that resistance was sincere.

Further consultations followed.

Rather than withdrawing the instruction, the reported communications gave it religious justification. The men were being asked to regard their ordinary understanding of marriage and morality as subordinate to a higher divine command.

What would ordinarily be considered sinful could, according to the reasoning presented to them, become permissible if God commanded it.

It was an exceptionally dangerous theological proposition.

Once accepted, it rendered virtually any action defensible, provided someone could establish that its author was divine.

The Pact

The two women at the center of the affair were Jane Dee and Jane Kelley.

Their voices reach us only imperfectly through the surviving historical records, which were principally written by the men.

Jane Dee, in particular, had responsibilities far removed from angelic hierarchies and cosmological speculation. She was raising children and maintaining a household under difficult circumstances.

Nevertheless, on May 3, 1587, the four entered into a written agreement concerning the proposed arrangement.

The pact invoked God and presented the union of the households as an undertaking founded upon charity, friendship and shared possessions.

Later entries indicate that the arrangement was acted upon in May, in an episode sometimes referred to as the cross-matching.

The precise circumstances remain uncertain. The existence of a written agreement should not be mistaken for evidence that all four participants consented freely or enthusiastically.

Nor should we overlook the enormous pressure that could be exerted upon a person when an unwanted proposition was presented not as the desire of another human being, but as the will of God.

The End of an Extraordinary Partnership

The collaboration that had produced years of elaborate spiritual material was approaching its conclusion.

Dee and Kelley eventually separated. Dee returned to England in 1589, while Kelley remained on the Continent, pursuing alchemy and aristocratic patronage.

Kelley's reputation would bring him both wealth and imprisonment.

Dee would continue his interests in spiritual communication, but his remarkable partnership with Kelley was over.

Their records, however, survived.

In 1659, the English scholar Meric Casaubon published a substantial collection of Dee's spiritual diaries under the title A True and Faithful Relation of What Passed for Many Yeers Between Dr. John Dee and Some Spirits.

Casaubon interpreted the material as evidence of deception by evil spirits.

Centuries later, occultists would revisit much of the same material and incorporate elements of it into influential systems of ceremonial magic.

One generation's evidence of demonic deception became another generation's source of ritual instruction.

Such is the complicated inheritance of John Dee.

The esoteric tradition

What is Enochian magic?

The spiritual investigations of Dee and Kelley form the historical foundation of what is now commonly called Enochian magic.

The name refers to the biblical patriarch Enoch, whose association with heavenly mysteries made him an important figure in the Western esoteric imagination.

The material received by Dee and Kelley includes:

  • An elaborate angelic language.
  • Tables of letters and divine names.
  • Hierarchies of spiritual intelligences.
  • Ritual instruments, seals and sacred diagrams.
  • Cosmological teachings concerning the organization of creation.

Dee's undertaking can be understood within the broader Renaissance pursuit of primordial wisdom: the belief that humanity once possessed knowledge of divine and natural mysteries that had subsequently been obscured or lost.

The magician sought to recover that knowledge.

This aspiration bears a philosophical resemblance to themes found elsewhere in Hermeticism, Rosicrucianism and esoteric Freemasonry.

The recovery of that which was lost.

During the nineteenth century, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn incorporated and reorganized portions of the Dee and Kelley material within its own system of ceremonial magic.

Later practitioners, including Aleister Crowley, developed it further.

Modern Enochian magic is therefore not simply an unchanged reproduction of Dee's original experiments. It is also the product of centuries of interpretation, reconstruction and magical practice.

The practitioner's perspective

The danger of mistaking revelation for authority

As a practitioner of ceremonial magic, what interests me most about this episode is not whether John Dee was communicating with angels.

It is the question of how he could possibly have known.

Suppose we entertain three possibilities.

  1. Kelley was deliberately manipulating Dee.
  2. Kelley was sincerely experiencing communications originating within his own psyche.
  3. Or Kelley was encountering something genuinely independent of himself.

Under any of these circumstances, the magician still faces precisely the same problem: discernment.

The mere appearance of an intelligence beyond our ordinary consciousness tells us nothing conclusive about its wisdom, benevolence or authority.

A being that possesses knowledge you do not possess is not necessarily your superior in matters of morality.

A remarkable experience is not necessarily a divine experience.

And an instruction received through a magical operation does not relieve the magician of responsibility for acting upon it.

There is an interesting parallel here with the symbolism of the High Priestess in the Hermetic Tarot.

The High Priestess sits between the pillars, before the veil. She occupies a position of equilibrium at the threshold of the mysteries.

Her proximity to the unseen does not require her to abandon that equilibrium.

Perhaps one of Dee's greatest errors was not his attempt to penetrate the mysteries of creation, but his willingness to allow the purported inhabitants of those mysteries to determine his conduct.

The magician must retain sovereignty over his own actions.

Otherwise, the pursuit of hidden knowledge becomes the surrender of judgment.

If an angel must first persuade you to abandon your conscience before imparting its wisdom, perhaps the first mystery worth investigating is the identity of the angel.
