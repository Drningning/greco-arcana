In 1785, one of Europe's most celebrated magicians was imprisoned in the Bastille over a diamond necklace that the Queen of France had never purchased.

The accused was an alchemist, healer, travelling occultist and founder of an extraordinary system of Freemasonry that promised the regeneration of man.

His name was Count Alessandro di Cagliostro.

Whether he was an enlightened initiate or one of the eighteenth century's most accomplished impostors remains a subject of debate. What is beyond dispute is that his life intersected with one of the greatest political scandals in European history.

And for once, the magician appears to have been among those innocent of the crime.

The man with an uncertain past

{{figure:lorenza|right|Lorenza Feliciani|Cagliostro's wife and travelling companion.}}

The man known as Count Cagliostro is generally identified by historians as Giuseppe Balsamo, born in Palermo, Sicily, in 1743.

His own accounts of his origins were considerably more mysterious.

Travelling throughout Europe with his wife, Lorenza Feliciani, Cagliostro acquired a reputation as a healer, alchemist and possessor of extraordinary knowledge. His supporters credited him with remarkable abilities. His detractors regarded him as a charlatan who had mastered the weaknesses of wealthy and ambitious people.

Between these two reputations stood a man who understood something about eighteenth-century Europe: its aristocracy possessed enormous material wealth, but many of its members remained hungry for spiritual knowledge.

Cagliostro offered them the promise of access to the ancient mysteries.

The Egyptian Rite

Among Cagliostro's most significant undertakings was the establishment of his Egyptian system of Freemasonry.

The eighteenth century was fertile ground for such an enterprise. Freemasonry was spreading across Europe, while Hermetic philosophy, alchemy, Rosicrucianism and the search for ancient initiatory wisdom attracted considerable attention.

Cagliostro presented Egyptian Masonry as something more than a system of moral instruction.

His teachings concerned the purification and regeneration of the individual, extending to spiritual and bodily renewal. His system also admitted women through associated lodges, distinguishing it from much of the Masonic culture of his period.

Its Egyptian pedigree, however, should not be confused with documented transmission from the temples of ancient Egypt.

What Cagliostro created belonged to the eighteenth-century European fascination with Egypt as a repository of primordial wisdom.

Was he restoring something ancient, constructing something new, or doing what occultists had done for centuries: expressing inherited philosophical ideas through a new initiatory framework?

The question remains remarkably relevant.

A necklace fit for a queen

{{figure:necklace|right|The diamond necklace|An eighteenth-century engraving of the necklace made by Böhmer and Bassenge.}}

The diamond necklace at the center of the affair had originally been intended for Madame du Barry, the mistress of Louis XV.

Containing hundreds of diamonds, it represented an extraordinary investment by the jewelers Charles Auguste Böhmer and Paul Bassenge.

When Louis XV died in 1774, the jewelers were left with an exceptionally expensive piece of jewelry and no purchaser.

They subsequently approached Marie Antoinette.

She declined.

Unfortunately for the French monarchy, another woman saw an opportunity in the unwanted necklace.

Her name was Jeanne de Valois-Saint-Rémy, better known as the Comtesse de la Motte.

The deception

Jeanne cultivated a relationship with Cardinal Louis de Rohan, a powerful churchman who desired restoration to the queen's favor.

Through forged correspondence, she convinced him that Marie Antoinette secretly wished to reconcile with him.

An impersonator was even employed to play the queen during a clandestine encounter.

Rohan eventually became convinced that Marie Antoinette wished to acquire the diamond necklace discreetly and that he could demonstrate his loyalty by facilitating the transaction.

The jewelers surrendered the necklace through arrangements involving the cardinal.

Jeanne and her associates obtained the diamonds, which were subsequently removed from their settings and dispersed.

The queen knew nothing of the arrangement.

When payment failed to materialize, the deception began to unravel.

Enter the magician

Cardinal de Rohan was acquainted with Cagliostro and had been among those attracted to his reputation and teachings.

That association was enough to bring the magician into the investigation.

Cagliostro was arrested in August 1785 and imprisoned in the Bastille alongside others implicated in the affair.

The circumstances were extraordinary.

A cardinal of the Roman Catholic Church, an aristocratic confidence trickster, a royal impersonator and an internationally renowned occultist had become central figures in an investigation involving the reputation of the Queen of France.

It reads like the plot of an extravagant historical novel.

It happened.

Cagliostro maintained his innocence, defending himself publicly through legal memorials circulated during the proceedings.

His reputation as a magician ensured that the accusations against him attracted considerable attention.

But reputation is not evidence.

The verdict

On May 31, 1786, the Parlement of Paris delivered its judgment.

Cardinal de Rohan was acquitted.

So was Cagliostro.

Jeanne de la Motte, by contrast, was convicted for her central involvement in the fraud.

The public response exposed the growing distance between the French monarchy and its subjects.

Although Marie Antoinette had been a victim of the deception, the scandal further damaged her reputation. Popular hostility toward royal extravagance proved more powerful than the particulars of the case.

Cagliostro was released, but royal authority required him to leave France.

The magician had survived the Bastille.

The monarchy itself would prove considerably less fortunate.

Within a few years, France would enter its Revolution.

The final imprisonment

Cagliostro's freedom did not last.

Returning to Italy, he eventually fell into the hands of the Roman Inquisition, which proceeded against him in connection with Freemasonry and his religious and occult activities.

His death sentence was commuted to perpetual imprisonment.

He died in 1795 at the fortress of San Leo.

The man who had promised spiritual regeneration and whose reputation extended across Europe ended his life confined within a prison.

His Egyptian Masonic system, however, survived him in various forms of historical and esoteric interest.

His acquittal in the Diamond Necklace Affair does not establish his supernatural abilities. Equally, doubts concerning his supernatural claims do not establish his guilt in a criminal conspiracy.

The esoteric tradition

The esoteric tradition behind the story

Cagliostro's significance extends beyond the extraordinary circumstances of his life.

His Egyptian Rite belongs to a period when European initiatory societies were exploring the relationship between Freemasonry and the greater Western Mystery Tradition.

Alchemy concerned the purification and transformation of imperfect substances.

Rosicrucian writings concerned the regeneration of humanity and the recovery of wisdom.

Hermetic philosophy investigated the relationship between man and the cosmos.

Freemasonry expressed moral and philosophical development through the symbolism of architecture and initiation.

Cagliostro attempted to bring several of these currents together within a system of ritual and spiritual development.

His work therefore illustrates an important historical phenomenon: the desire to move beyond the explanation of esoteric principles toward their practical application.

Whether his extraordinary promises were attainable is another question entirely.

A practitioner's perspective

There is a distinction that students of the mysteries would do well to remember.

Possessing knowledge of spiritual transformation is not the same as having undergone it.

An individual may understand the symbolism of alchemy without having purified his own character. He may possess the vocabulary of initiation without having developed the qualities initiation is intended to cultivate.

He may even become so accomplished at presenting the appearance of wisdom that others mistake the appearance for the substance.

Cagliostro provides us with a particularly difficult figure through whom to investigate this distinction.

His acquittal in the Diamond Necklace Affair does not establish his supernatural abilities. Equally, doubts concerning his supernatural claims do not establish his guilt in a criminal conspiracy.

Each question must be examined on its own merits.

This is part of the discipline of esoteric study.

The student must cultivate an imagination capable of recognizing possibilities beyond ordinary appearances, while maintaining sufficient judgment to distinguish possibility from evidence.

Perhaps the most valuable lesson Cagliostro offers us is not contained in one of his rituals.

Perhaps it is contained in the contradictory reputation he left behind.

The mysteries deserve more than blind belief.

They also deserve more than blind dismissal.

The archive remains open

Cagliostro's life raises questions that continue to confront the Western Mystery Tradition.

What constitutes authentic initiation?

Can a system be spiritually valuable without possessing the ancient lineage it claims?

How does the student distinguish an accomplished practitioner from an accomplished performer?

And what evidence should we demand before accepting extraordinary claims?

More than two centuries after Cagliostro's death, these remain questions worth asking.
