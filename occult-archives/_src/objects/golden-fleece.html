§ I

The artifact

{{figure:nolin|right}}

Among the treasures of antiquity, few inspired an expedition as ambitious as the Golden Fleece.

Kings, warriors, demigods and celebrated heroes assembled aboard a vessel called the Argo. Among them were Heracles, Orpheus and the twin brothers Castor and Pollux.

Their destination was a kingdom on the eastern shores of the Black Sea.

Their objective was the preserved hide of a supernatural ram.

The fleece was originally associated with Phrixus and his sister Helle, who escaped danger upon the back of a miraculous flying ram.

Phrixus reached the kingdom of Colchis, where the animal was sacrificed and its extraordinary fleece entrusted to King Aeëtes.

The king placed the treasure within a sacred grove dedicated to Ares. There, it was suspended from an oak tree and guarded by a serpent or dragon that never slept.

Generations of treasure seekers have wondered whether the legend preserves the memory of an extraordinary object that once existed in the ancient kingdoms of the Caucasus.

Unlike many mythological treasures, the Golden Fleece has a surprisingly plausible geographical and material connection to the historical world.

§ II

The last known whereabouts

The original custodian of the fleece was Aeëtes, the legendary king of Colchis.

Ancient Colchis occupied territory corresponding broadly to modern western Georgia, along the eastern shores of the Black Sea.

The Argonautica of Apollonius of Rhodes provides an extensive account of Jason's expedition to obtain the treasure and return with it to Greece.

During the return voyage, the fleece appears again at the marriage of Jason and Medea, where it is spread upon their wedding couch.

The expedition eventually returns to its homeland. Beyond the fleece's return to Greece, however, no reliable historical chain of possession exists.

§ III · Evidence assembled

The clues

IOracle and poetry

Clue I: The man with one sandal

Jason's expedition begins with an oracle.

King Pelias had been warned to fear a man who approached him wearing only one sandal.

Before appearing before the king, Jason loses a sandal while crossing a river.

In the account of Apollonius, the goddess Hera remembers Jason favorably because he once helped her cross a dangerous river while she was disguised as an elderly woman.

His apparent misfortune becomes the identifying mark of the man destined to undertake the expedition.

The first clue is therefore associated not with the object, but with the identity and character of its seeker.

Sources: Pindar, Pythian IV; Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica

IILiterary testimony

Clue II: The object was kept in a sacred grove

The fleece was not displayed in the king's palace or buried inside a treasury.

It was placed upon an oak within a grove sacred to Ares.

The association with a particular tree, deity and guardian suggests that its placement carried religious significance.

If the legend preserves something of an ancient ritual practice, the original object may have belonged to a religious sanctuary rather than an ordinary royal collection.

Source: Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica

IIILiterary testimony

Clue III: The three trials

Before Jason could claim the fleece, Aeëtes imposed seemingly impossible tasks.

He was required to:

  • Master supernatural bulls that breathed fire.
  • Plow a field and sow the teeth of a dragon.
  • Overcome the armed warriors that emerged from the earth.

Jason could not accomplish these tasks through physical strength alone.

He required knowledge of magical substances, knowledge of the trials and assistance from someone familiar with their mysteries.

Source: Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica, Book III

IVLiterary testimony

Clue IV: Seek the priestess of Hecate

Jason's most important ally was Medea, daughter of Aeëtes and a practitioner of magic associated with the goddess Hecate.

Her knowledge allowed him to survive the king's trials and approach the fleece.

When they reached the sacred grove, Medea used her magical arts against its sleepless guardian.

Jason acquired the treasure while the guardian was overcome by her enchantments.

The most celebrated heroes of Greece had assembled to recover the Golden Fleece.

Yet it was a magician who ultimately made its acquisition possible.

Source: Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica, Books III and IV

VHistorical geography

Clue V: The historical explanation

Writing around the beginning of the Common Era, the Greek geographer Strabo supplied an extraordinary clue.

He described inhabitants of the Caucasus collecting gold carried downstream by mountain rivers, using equipment that included animal fleeces.

Particles of gold became trapped within the wool.

Strabo himself connected this practice with the origin of the Golden Fleece legend.

This does not establish that Jason's particular fleece existed.

However, it demonstrates that an ancient author identified a plausible material origin for the very object that had become the subject of Greek mythology.

Could stories concerning the wealth of Colchis have preserved the memory of an exceptional sacred treasure?

Source: Strabo, Geography, XI.2.19

§ V

The path to attainment

{{figure:boyvin|wide}}

The story suggests that the treasure could not be obtained through a single heroic accomplishment.

Jason needed courage to undertake the expedition, intelligence to overcome its obstacles, assistance from others and knowledge of magical arts.

His trials brought him into contact with fire, earth, water and supernatural forces.

He was ultimately required to approach a sacred place and overcome its guardian.

From an initiatory perspective, the structure of the expedition is remarkably familiar.

The seeker departs the ordinary world, undergoes trials, acquires knowledge, encounters a guardian and returns bearing something previously inaccessible to him.

§ VI · Attributed to Joseph Greco

The practitioner's notes

What fascinates me about the Golden Fleece is that the greatest warriors in Greece were insufficient to obtain it.

Jason had Heracles among his company during the earlier portion of the expedition. He had great warriors, exceptional navigators and men descended from the gods.

Yet the final acquisition depended upon knowledge that none of their physical abilities could replace.

There is an interesting parallel here with alchemy.

The gold described by Strabo already existed within the waters passing through the mountains. The fleece was an instrument through which that gold could be separated from everything accompanying it.

The gold was present, but ordinarily inaccessible.

What if the fleece represents the instrument through which something valuable, already present within nature, can be isolated and made manifest?

When viewed through this lens, the story acquires an entirely different dimension.

Jason's expedition concerns the acquisition of gold. But the knowledge necessary to acquire it becomes considerably more valuable than the gold itself.

Perhaps the greatest treasure aboard the Argo on its return voyage was not the Golden Fleece, but the knowledge of how it was attained.
