Excalibur

The sword that came from the waters.

§ I

The artifact

Somewhere in the waters of Britain, according to one of its most enduring legends, lies a sword that was never intended to remain in the possession of men.

It belonged to a king, but its origins preceded his ownership.

It was given to him by a mysterious woman associated with a body of water, and when his reign came to an end, it was demanded back.

The sword was Excalibur.

Most people associate Excalibur with the famous story of Arthur pulling a sword from a stone. Yet the medieval accounts are considerably more complicated.

In Thomas Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, Arthur receives a sword from the Lady of the Lake after breaking his earlier weapon.

The distinction matters enormously to anyone investigating the disappearance of Excalibur.

We may be dealing with two separate swords whose identities became intertwined over centuries of storytelling.

§ II

The last known whereabouts

Following the Battle of Camlann, Arthur lies mortally wounded.

Recognizing that his reign has come to an end, he instructs Sir Bedivere to take Excalibur to the water and cast it into the depths.

Bedivere hesitates.

Twice, he returns without fulfilling the instruction, unable to bring himself to discard such an extraordinary treasure.

On his third attempt, he finally throws the sword.

A mysterious arm emerges from the water, catches Excalibur, brandishes it three times and disappears beneath the surface.

No subsequent possessor of the sword is established in Malory's account.

The treasure had returned to that which originally bestowed it.

§ III · Evidence assembled

The clues

IChronicle testimony

Clue I: Forged in Avalon

Geoffrey of Monmouth, writing in the twelfth century, identifies Arthur's sword as Caliburn and associates its manufacture with the island of Avalon.

Avalon itself remains one of the great geographical mysteries of Arthurian literature.

If Geoffrey's account preserves an earlier tradition, perhaps the search for Excalibur begins with the search for the place in which it was made.

Source: Geoffrey of Monmouth, Historia Regum Britanniae, Book IX

IILiterary testimony

Clue II: The sword in the stone may be a different artifact

In Malory's narrative, Arthur breaks a sword before Merlin leads him to the lake where he receives Excalibur.

The sword in the stone establishes Arthur's claim to sovereignty. The sword from the lake is bestowed upon him after that claim has already been established.

The two episodes may represent separate stages of his development.

For the treasure hunter, the distinction is critical. Searching for a stone containing a sword might have very little to do with the whereabouts of Excalibur.

Source: Malory, Le Morte d'Arthur, Book I

IIILiterary testimony

Clue III: The woman of the waters

When Arthur approaches the lake, an arm bearing a sword rises above its surface.

The Lady of the Lake permits Arthur to acquire the weapon, but the gift comes with an obligation to grant her a future request.

The sword is not won through combat. It is received through an agreement with a mysterious custodian.

This raises an important question: was Arthur ever its owner, or merely its temporary bearer?

Source: Malory, Le Morte d'Arthur, Book I

IVLiterary testimony

Clue IV: The scabbard was more valuable than the sword

Merlin asks Arthur which he prefers: the sword or its scabbard.

Arthur chooses the sword.

Merlin explains that the scabbard is considerably more valuable because its wearer will not lose blood while carrying it, however severely wounded.

The scabbard is subsequently taken through the machinations of Morgan le Fay and eventually cast into water.

There are therefore potentially two extraordinary artifacts to investigate, not one.

And according to Merlin, we may have spent centuries searching for the less valuable of the two.

Sources: Malory, Le Morte d'Arthur, Books I and IV

VLiterary testimony

Clue V: The sword must be surrendered willingly

Bedivere's unwillingness to relinquish Excalibur becomes a test in its own right.

Only after overcoming his attachment to the treasure does he witness its supernatural retrieval.

Curiously, the same waters that granted the sword also receive it.

The final guardian may be the same mysterious power that originally authorized its possession.

Source: Malory, Le Morte d'Arthur, Book XXI

§ IV

Where might Excalibur be?

Several British locations have become associated with the Arthurian legend.

The most intriguing candidates include:

LocationAssociation
Dozmary Pool, CornwallA traditional location for Excalibur's return to the lake
Loe Pool, CornwallAnother lake associated with the sword's disappearance
Glastonbury, SomersetHistorically associated with the legendary Avalon
Llyn Llydaw, WalesAssociated in regional traditions with Arthur and his final journey

None of these locations has produced an authenticated Excalibur. They represent geographical traditions worthy of literary investigation, not verified archaeological leads.

§ V

The path to attainment

The Arthurian accounts suggest that Excalibur cannot simply be won by the strongest warrior.

Arthur first establishes his legitimacy as king.

He then receives the sword from a power outside his kingdom, through an agreement that carries obligations.

When his reign ends, the sword must be returned.

The pattern is remarkably different from that of an ordinary treasure.

Sovereignty precedes possession. Possession carries responsibility. When that responsibility ends, the artifact is surrendered.

§ VI · Attributed to Joseph Greco

The practitioner's notes

The sword occupies a particularly important position within the Western Mystery Tradition.

In many systems of ceremonial magic, it is associated with discrimination, intellect, authority and the ability to establish boundaries.

Consider the distinction between Arthur's two swords.

The first establishes who he is.

The second provides him with an instrument through which to exercise what he has become.

This distinction appears repeatedly in initiatory literature. An instrument of power is bestowed after some corresponding quality has already been established within its recipient.

There is also something extraordinary about Merlin's preference for the scabbard.

Arthur values the instrument through which he can impose his will upon the world.

Merlin values that which preserves him while he does so.

Perhaps the magician should take notice.

The wisdom to contain power may be more valuable than the power itself.
