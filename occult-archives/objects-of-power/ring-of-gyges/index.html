The Ring of Gyges

The first man we know to have worn it was already dead.

By Joseph Greco·24 November 2026·2 min read

The object

In Plato’s Republic, an earthquake opens a fissure beneath a Lydian shepherd’s grazing ground. He climbs down and finds a hollow bronze horse. Inside lies a body larger than an ordinary man, with a gold ring on its hand. The shepherd takes it. Later, among his fellow shepherds, he turns the setting inward and hears them begin to speak as though he has left the room.

When it vanished

No disappearance date survives. The trail ends inside a story written down in the fourth century BCE. Last known holder in the story: the shepherd in Lydia, in western Anatolia, who uses the ring to seize the throne. Plato’s account calls him an ancestor of Gyges.

The clues

The ring has a precise operating instruction. Turn the setting inward and you vanish. Turn it outward and you return. The hiding place is just as precise: a fissure, a bronze horse with openings, an oversized corpse. The shepherd takes the ring straight from the dead man’s hand. Whoever put that body inside the horse is the person I’d want to find.

How to pursue it

Start with the bronze horse. Search early versions and retellings for the combination of a hollow horse, a body, and a ring. A gold ring can turn up anywhere. That particular burial is the clue specific enough to follow. Then try Plato’s test on yourself. If you could turn the setting inward for one afternoon, what would you do first? How long did it take you to think of it?
